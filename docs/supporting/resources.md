# Resources and References

Curated references for bootstrapping industrial civilization from first principles. Each entry explains *why* it matters for the tech tree, not just what it covers. Paywalled sources are marked [paywall].

## Machine Tools and Foundry Work

- Dave Gingery, *Build Your Own Metal Working Shop from Scrap* (7-volume series). The canonical self-replicating machine tool series. Starts with a charcoal-fired aluminum foundry (Vol 1) and bootstraps a lathe (Vol 2), shaper (Vol 3), mill (Vol 4), drill press (Vol 5), dividing head (Vol 6), and sheet metal brake (Vol 7). Each machine builds the next, demonstrating the core iterative principle of the entire tech tree.
- L. T. C. Rolt, *Tools for the Job* (1965). Narrative history of machine tool development from Maudslay through Nasmyth to precision grinding. Useful for understanding *why* each tool category emerged and what manufacturing bottleneck it solved.
- C. F. Carter, *Practical Ironfounding* (1964). Covers patternmaking, core boxes, gating systems, and defect diagnosis for sand casting. The foundry is the entry point for producing complex metal shapes without a machine shop, making this a day-one reference.
- Philip S. Taddei, *Machine Shop Trade Secrets* (2003). Practical workholding, fixturing, and measurement shortcuts from a career machinist. Bridges the gap between textbook theory and the tacit knowledge that makes machine work productive.
- Lucien Ingraham, *Machine Tool Operations* (2 vol.). Standard vocational textbook covering lathe, mill, grinder, and drill press operations with setup procedures. Good structured reference for basic operations when no journeyman is available to teach.

## Metallurgy and Materials

- R. F. Tylecote, *A History of Metallurgy* (1992). The definitive archaeological and historical survey of metalworking from native copper through alloy steels. Essential for understanding which ores, furnaces, and techniques are reachable at each tech level.
- Georgius Agricola, *De Re Metallica* (1556), trans. Hoover. Renaissance-era mining, assaying, smelting, and refining described in enough detail to reproduce. Remarkably complete, covering everything from mine timbering to parting gold from silver. The 1912 Hoover translation includes helpful footnotes.
- John D. Verhoeven, *Steel Metallurgy for the Non-Metallurgist* (2007). Explains heat treatment, phase diagrams, and the relationship between carbon content, cooling rate, and resulting hardness. Critical for producing reliable tool steel, spring steel, and structural steel from a basic furnace.
- W. H. Dennis, *Metallurgy in the Service of Man* (1961). Broad survey connecting ore deposits, extraction methods, and end-use applications. Helpful for the big-picture view of which metals become available at which stage of development.
- Arthur Street and William Alexander, *Metals in the Service of Man* (1944). Earlier edition of the above tradition, with more detail on non-ferrous metals (copper, tin, zinc, lead). Useful for the pre-steel metallurgy sequence.

## Agriculture

- Jack R. Harlan, *Crops and Man* (1992). Foundational text on where our crop species came from, how they were domesticated, and what genetic diversity remains. Answers the question "which plants should I cultivate first?" with deep biological grounding.
- Vaclav Smil, *Enriching the Earth: Fritz Haber, Carl Bosch, and the Transformation of World Food Production* (2001). The story of synthetic nitrogen fertilizer: why it matters (half of all human bodies contain nitrogen fixed by the Haber-Bosch process), how it works, and what industrial base is required. Directly relevant to the chemistry and energy prerequisites in the tech tree.
- David R. Montgomery, *Dirt: The Erosion of Civilizations* (2007). Soil is the non-renewable resource that kills civilizations when it washes away. This book explains conservation agriculture, terracing, and why "grow food first, build industry second" fails without soil management.
- J. Russell Smith, *Tree Crops: A Permanent Agriculture* (1929). Argues for perennial tree-based agriculture (chestnut, oak, persimmon, honey locust) as an alternative to annual grain monoculture. Relevant for low-input, low-labor food systems that free up workers for industrial tasks.
- Gary Nabhan, *Where Our Food Comes From* (2009). Traces Vavilov's seed-collecting journeys and the geography of crop diversity. Useful for understanding which crops suit which climates and latitudes.
- FAO, *Soil Survey Manual* (2017, free online). Practical field guide to reading soil: texture, structure, pH, drainage, and fertility. A civilization that can't assess its soil can't plan its agriculture.

## Construction

- J. E. Gordon, *Structures: Or Why Things Don't Fall Down* (1978). The most accessible book ever written on structural engineering. Explains tension, compression, shear, and buckling using everyday examples. Required reading before building bridges, roofs, or multi-story buildings.
- J. E. Gordon, *The New Science of Strong Materials, or Why You Don't Fall Through the Floor* (1968). Companion volume focused on material properties: why steel is strong, why glass is brittle, why wood is tough. Connects materials science to the structural decisions builders face.
- Edward Allen and Joseph Iano, *Fundamentals of Building Construction: Materials and Methods* (7th ed., 2019) [paywall]. Comprehensive textbook covering foundations, framing, masonry, concrete, steel, and cladding. Good structured reference for construction methods at every scale from shed to factory.
- Vitruvius, *De Architectura* (1st c. BC). Classical Roman construction manual covering materials (timber, brick, stone, concrete), structural principles, and site planning. Remarkably practical; the sections on lime mortar and concrete are directly reproducible.
- Hardy Cross, "Analysis of Continuous Frames by Distributing Fixed-End Moments" (1930). The moment distribution method made structural analysis of indeterminate structures tractable by hand calculation. Reprinted in many structural analysis textbooks. Essential for designing multi-span beams and rigid frames without a computer.

## Vacuum Technology

- John F. O'Hanlon, *A User's Guide to Vacuum Technology* (3rd ed., 2003) [paywall]. The go-to practical reference for vacuum system design: pump selection, seal materials, outgassing rates, leak detection, and pressure measurement. Written for engineers who need to build and maintain systems, not just theorize about them.
- A. Roth, *Vacuum Technology* (3rd ed., 1990). Comprehensive but approachable. Covers the full range from rough vacuum (mechanical pumps) through high vacuum (diffusion pumps, cryopumps) to ultra-high vacuum. Includes useful data tables for gas conductance, vapor pressures, and material compatibility.
- Marsbed H. Hablanian, *High-Vacuum Technology: A Practical Guide* (2nd ed., 1997). Focuses on diffusion pumps, turbomolecular pumps, and cryopumps with enough construction detail to inform design decisions. The chapter on pump-down calculations is directly useful for sizing vacuum chambers for semiconductor processing.
- Nigel S. Harris, *Modern Vacuum Practice* (3rd ed., 2005). Shorter and more hands-on than O'Hanlon. Good for quick reference on flange types, bakeout procedures, and residual gas analysis.
- P. A. Redhead, "The Ultimate Vacuum" in *Vacuum*, vol. 50, no. 2, pp. 77-95 (1998). Historical survey of how vacuum limits were pushed from Torricelli's barometer through modern UHV. Helps set realistic expectations for what's achievable at each tech level.

## Precision Engineering

- Wayne R. Moore, *Foundations of Mechanical Accuracy* (1970). The single most important book on precision machine design. Moore explains how the Moore Special Tool Company achieved sub-micron accuracy using geometric principles (the Abbé principle, squareness through reversal, flatness through three-plate scraping) rather than expensive materials. Many techniques are reproducible with patience and cast iron. Out of print but widely scanned.
- Hume, *Precision Engineering* (1970). Traces the historical development of dimensional accuracy from Watt's micrometer through block gauges and optical flats. Shows that precision is a cumulative process: each generation of better measuring tools enables the next generation of better machines.
- V. V. Nalimov, *Precision Measurement* series. Soviet-era precision engineering texts emphasizing achievable accuracy with simple equipment. The "reversal" and "substitution" measurement techniques described are powerful ways to extract nanometer-level information from micron-level instruments.
- Jim Bryan, "The Abbé Principle Revisited: An Updated Interpretation" in *Precision Engineering*, vol. 1, no. 3, pp. 129-132 (1979). Clarifies when Abbé errors matter and when they don't. Critical for lathe and jig borer design, where the relationship between the measurement axis and the cutting axis determines achievable accuracy.
- Richard R. Baldwin, *Precision Grinding* (1963). Covers surface grinding, cylindrical grinding, and tool-and-cutter grinding with practical setup instructions. Grinding is the process that turns a "good" machine tool into a "precision" one.

## Cleanrooms and Contamination Control

- William Whyte, *Cleanroom Technology: Fundamentals of Design, Testing and Operation* (2nd ed., 2022). The cleanroom equivalent of O'Hanlon for vacuum: practical, comprehensive, and aimed at working engineers. Covers airflow design, filter selection (HEPA/ULPA), garmenting protocols, and particle monitoring. Directly applicable to designing a semiconductor fab from scratch.
- Anne Marie Dixon, *Cleanroom Management and Technology* (2005). Shorter handbook format with checklists and procedures for cleanroom operation, cleaning, and validation. Useful for the "day-to-day running" problem once a cleanroom exists.
- ISO 14644 series (available from ISO, partial drafts circulated freely). The international standard defining cleanroom classifications (ISO 1 through ISO 9), testing methods, and operational requirements. Even partial familiarity with 14644-1 (classification) and 14644-3 (test methods) is valuable for setting spec targets.
- T. A. Kletz, *What Went Wrong? Case Histories of Process Plant Disasters and How They Could Have Been Avoided* (5th ed., 2009). Not cleanroom-specific, but essential reading on contamination and procedural failures in high-purity process environments. The mental model "contamination kills products" is taught more effectively through failure stories than through specifications.
- Philip R. Austin, *Design and Operation of Clean Rooms* (1970). Older but focused on the fundamentals: air changes, pressure cascades, and laminar flow principles that haven't changed. Useful when designing with limited materials, since it pre-dates the era of modular cleanroom panels and disposable garments.

## Semiconductor Fabrication

- James D. Plummer, Michael Deal, and Peter B. Griffin, *Silicon VLSI Technology: Fundamentals, Practice, and Modeling* (2000) [paywall]. The one-book library for silicon device fabrication. Covers crystal growth, oxidation, diffusion, ion implantation, deposition, lithography, etching, and process integration. Written at a level that assumes physics and chemistry but not semiconductor experience.
- Peter Van Zant, *Microchip Fabrication: A Practical Guide to Semiconductor Processing* (6th ed., 2014). More accessible than Plummer et al., with less math and more process description. Good starting point for understanding what a fab actually *does* before diving into the physics.
- S. M. Sze and Ming-Kwei Lee, *Semiconductor Devices: Physics and Technology* (3rd ed., 2012) [paywall]. Device physics textbook connecting process choices to electrical outcomes. Necessary for understanding why a particular doping profile or gate oxide thickness matters, not just how to achieve it.
- Michael Riordan and Lillian Hoddeson, *Crystal Fire: The Birth of the Information Age* (1997). Narrative history of the transistor's invention and the early semiconductor industry. Useful for understanding the sequence of discoveries and what laboratory capabilities were needed at each stage.
- Robert P. Feytis, "The Making of the First Silicon Solar Cell" in *Journal of the Electrochemical Society*. Historical account of the practical steps and failures in early silicon photovoltaics. Directly relevant to the tech tree's solar-grade silicon pathway.
- Christophe Lécuyer, *Making Silicon Valley: Innovation and the Growth of High Tech, 1930-1970* (2006). Shows how the semiconductor ecosystem grew from vacuum tubes through transistors to integrated circuits, including the supporting industries (chemicals, equipment, measurement) that had to emerge alongside the devices.

## Metrology and Measurement

- Thomas G. Beckwith, Roy D. Marangoni, and John H. Lienhard, *Mechanical Measurements* (6th ed., 2007) [paywall]. Standard textbook covering dimensional measurement, force measurement, temperature measurement, and pressure measurement. The chapters on error analysis and uncertainty propagation are essential for any precision measurement program.
- Ernest O. Doebelin, *Measurement Systems: Application and Design* (5th ed., 2004) [paywall]. Broad survey of transducer principles and signal conditioning. Useful for understanding how to convert physical quantities into readable signals, from thermocouples to strain gauges to LVDTs.
- R. E. Reason, *Measurement of Surface Texture* (1970). The definitive work on surface finish measurement: why it matters, how to specify it, and how to measure it with stylus instruments. Surface finish is the hidden variable that determines whether bearings last, seals hold, and optical surfaces image cleanly.
- Galya ElMaraghy, *Flexible and Reconfigurable Manufacturing Systems* (2008). Contains a good chapter on in-process measurement and adaptive control. Relevant for understanding how measurement feeds back into manufacturing process control.
- Hume, *Engineering Metrology* (2nd ed., 1963). Practical handbook of dimensional measurement techniques: gauge blocks, sine bars, autocollimators, and optical flats. Many of these instruments can be built or calibrated using techniques described in Moore's *Foundations of Mechanical Accuracy*.

## Chemistry and Chemical Engineering

- Trevor I. Williams, *A History of Chemical Technology* (1957). Chronological survey from ancient dyestuffs and glass through Le Blanc, Solvay, Haber-Bosch, and petrochemicals. Good for understanding the sequence in which chemical processes became available and what feedstocks each requires.
- Ludwig Mond's papers on the Mond process for nickel carbonyl refining (late 1880s, widely reprinted). A case study in gas-phase chemical processing that also demonstrates how industrial chemistry bootstraps itself: nickel refining enables better catalysts, which enable better refining.
- Warren K. Lewis and Arthur B. Metzner, *Industrial Chemical Calculations* (1930s, various editions). Stoichiometry, mass balances, energy balances, and reactor sizing for common industrial processes. The quantitative backbone for scaling a bench reaction to a production plant.
- Robert Thornton, *The Chemical Industry* (1971). Broad survey of the major sectors: acids, alkalis, fertilizers, dyestuffs, explosives, and plastics. Maps out the interdependencies between chemical processes.

## Energy and Power

- Henry Dickenson, *A Short History of the Steam Engine* (1939). From Newcomen's atmospheric engine through Watt's separate condenser to Corliss and compound engines. Shows the iterative refinement process and the metallurgical challenges at each stage.
- Thomas P. Hughes, *Networks of Power: Electrification in Western Society, 1880-1930* (1983). How electrical grids grew from isolated generators to interconnected systems. Essential for understanding the infrastructure requirements (copper, transformers, switchgear, standards) that make electrical power useful beyond a single factory.
- Vaclav Smil, *Energy and Civilization: A History* (2017). Comprehensive survey of energy flows from human muscle through fossil fuels to nuclear. Provides the calorie-per-capita benchmarks that define what level of civilization is sustainable at each energy budget.
- A. P. Fraas, *Engineering Evaluation of Energy Systems* (1982). Thermodynamic analysis of power generation and conversion systems. Useful for comparing steam engines, gas turbines, internal combustion engines, and early electrical generation on a common efficiency basis.

## Glass and Optics

- W. E. S. Turner, *Elements of Glass Technology for Scientific and Industrial Workers* (1920). Practical glassmaking from batch composition through melting, forming, and annealing. Includes recipes for soda-lime, lead, and borosilicate glasses with the reasoning behind each ingredient.
- Frank Twyman, *Prism and Lens Making* (2nd ed., 1952). The standard reference on optical fabrication: grinding, polishing, figuring, and testing lenses, prisms, and flats. Many techniques (pitch laps, rouge polishing, knife-edge testing) are directly reproducible.
- A. N. Dobrovol'skii, *The Production of Optical Glass* (1960s, various translations). Covers melting, stirring, annealing, and quality control for optical-grade glass. Addresses the difficult problem of achieving homogeneity and freedom from striae, which is what separates optical glass from window glass.
- Denis G. G. Sliney and Marshall L. Wolbarsht, *Safety with Lasers and Other Optical Sources* (1980). While focused on safety, the chapters on optical radiation measurement and material damage thresholds are useful for understanding the practical limits of optical systems.

## General Civilizational Development

- Vaclav Smil, *Creating the Twentieth Century: Technical Innovations of 1867-1914 and Their Lasting Impact* (2005). The two generations that created modern industrial civilization: electricity, internal combustion, synthetic chemicals, steel, and aluminum. Demonstrates that the "takeoff" from pre-industrial to industrial requires a cluster of technologies, not a single invention.
- Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence: China, Europe, and the Making of the Modern World Economy* (2000). Comparative economic history asking why industrialization happened in Europe and not elsewhere. Useful for understanding which geographic and institutional factors accelerate or retard industrial development.
- Charles Singer et al., *A History of Technology* (8 vol., 1954-1984). Massive multivolume survey of every technology from stone tools through the mid-20th century. An expensive reference set [paywall in print], but library copies are widely available and individual chapters are self-contained.

## Online Resources

- [mermaid.live](https://mermaid.live) — Render and edit Mermaid diagrams interactively. Used for viewing the auto-generated tech tree diagrams in this project.
- [Periodic Videos](https://www.periodicvideos.com/) (University of Nottingham) — Video series demonstrating the chemistry of every element. Useful for building intuition about which elements behave how, especially the less common ones relevant to semiconductor processing.
- [Explain That Stuff](https://www.explainthatstuff.com/) — Accessible explanations of technology principles. Good for high-level overviews when you need to understand what a process does before diving into textbooks.
- [NIST Engineering Statistics Handbook](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/) (free online) — Practical statistics for measurement, process control, and experiment design. Critical for making manufacturing decisions based on data rather than guesswork.
- [OpenStax Chemistry](https://openstax.org/details/books/chemistry-2e) (free textbook) — Complete general chemistry textbook suitable for self-study. Covers the fundamentals needed before tackling industrial chemistry or materials science.
