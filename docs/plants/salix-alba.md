White willow (*Salix alba*) is a deciduous tree native to Europe and western Asia whose bark contains salicin, a glycoside that the body converts to salicylic acid. Salicylic acid is the active precursor to acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin), one of the most important medicines in history. Willow bark has been used for pain relief, fever reduction, and inflammation since ancient Egyptian, Greek, and Chinese medical traditions.

Salicin content in willow bark ranges from 0.5-1.5% by dry weight, concentrated in the inner bark (phloem). When ingested, salicin is hydrolyzed in the gut to saligenin (salicyl alcohol), which is then oxidized to salicylic acid. The therapeutic effect is similar to aspirin but slower in onset and gentler on the stomach, because the conversion happens gradually in the body rather than all at once.

Willow bark tea or decoction provides effective relief for headaches, muscle pain, joint inflammation, and fever. The standard dose is 1-3 grams of dried bark, boiled in water, yielding approximately 60-120 mg of salicin. This is comparable to a low-dose aspirin tablet (75-100 mg).

Beyond medicine, willow is an excellent basketry material. The long, flexible shoots (withies) can be woven into baskets, fish traps, hurdles, and fencing. Willow roots readily from cuttings and can be coppiced annually for a sustainable supply of withies. The tree grows rapidly (1-2 meters per year) in moist soils near waterways.

Primary outputs: `salicin` (pain relief medicine), `basketry_withies` for woven goods.

## Process Description

### Bark Harvest

1. Plant willow cuttings (30 cm long, 1-2 cm diameter) in moist soil in early spring. Space 0.5-1.0 meters apart for coppice, 3-5 meters for specimen trees. Cuttings root in 2-4 weeks.
2. Allow trees to grow for 2-3 years before first bark harvest. Coppice (cut to 10 cm above ground) to produce multiple straight shoots.
3. Harvest bark in spring (March-May) when the sap is rising and bark peels easily. Select 2-3 year old shoots. Cut the shoot and strip bark by making a lengthwise slit and peeling.
4. Dry bark strips in a warm, well-ventilated area for 5-10 days until brittle.
5. Grind dried bark to a coarse powder. Store in sealed containers away from light and moisture.

### Salicin Extraction and Use

1. **Willow bark tea (simple extraction)**: Add 1-3 g of dried bark powder to 250 ml of boiling water. Simmer for 10-15 minutes. Strain and drink. Provides approximately 5-30 mg of salicin per cup. Take 2-3 cups per day for pain or fever.
2. **Concentrated decoction**: Boil 30 g of dried bark in 500 ml of water until reduced to 250 ml. Strain. Take 15-30 ml (1-2 tablespoons) as a dose. Approximately 3-4 times more concentrated than simple tea.
3. **Salicylic acid extraction (higher technology)**: Hydrolyze salicin by boiling bark in dilute sulfuric acid (1-2%) for 2-4 hours. Neutralize with calcium carbonate. Filter. The solution contains free salicylic acid. Further purification requires recrystallization from hot water.

### Basketry Withies

1. Harvest 1-year-old shoots from coppiced willow in winter when leaves have fallen.
2. Sort by length and thickness. Soak dried withies in water for 3-7 days to make them pliable.
3. Weave into baskets, fences, or hurdles using established basketry techniques.

### Willow Bark Medicine Details

The pharmacology of willow bark centers on the conversion pathway: salicin → saligenin → salicylic acid. This multi-step conversion in the body provides a gradual onset of action (30-60 minutes) and longer duration (4-6 hours) compared to synthetic aspirin, which acts within 15-30 minutes but has a similar duration. The gradual conversion also makes willow bark gentler on the stomach than aspirin, because less salicylic acid is present in the stomach at any one time.

Salicin content varies between willow species. White willow (*S. alba*) contains 0.5-1.5%. Purple willow (*S. purpurea*) contains up to 3% and is the preferred species for medicine where available. Crack willow (*S. fragilis*) contains 1-2%. The bark of younger shoots (2-3 years) has higher salicin concentration than older bark. Spring-harvested bark has higher content than autumn bark.

For communities without access to synthetic aspirin, willow bark provides effective pain relief for headaches, dental pain, muscle soreness, menstrual cramps, and fever reduction. It is less effective for severe pain (broken bones, surgery) but adequate for the majority of everyday discomforts. The anti-inflammatory properties also make it useful for joint pain (arthritis) when taken regularly at moderate doses.

### Basketry and Withy Production

Willow withies are among the best basketry materials in the temperate zone. The shoots are long, flexible, uniform in diameter, and split easily. Willow baskets were the standard containers of pre-industrial Europe, used for everything from fishing traps to harvest baskets to laundry hampers. Willow is also woven into hurdles (temporary fencing panels), lobster pots, and beehive skeps.

For the best withies, coppice willow annually in winter. Sort shoots by length and thickness into grades. Withies for fine basketry should be 1-2 meters long and 3-6 mm in diameter. Soak dried withies in water for 3-7 days until pliable before weaving. Fresh green withies can be woven immediately but shrink as they dry, creating loose weave. Soaking dried withies and weaving them damp produces tighter work.

## Safety Considerations

- Willow bark should not be given to children under 16 with viral infections (risk of Reye's syndrome, same as aspirin).
- Avoid in people with aspirin allergy, bleeding disorders, or stomach ulcers. Salicylic acid inhibits blood clotting and irritates the gastric lining.
- Do not combine with blood-thinning medications. Willow bark increases bleeding risk.
- Prolonged use at high doses can cause tinnitus (ringing in ears), a sign of salicylate toxicity. Reduce dose or discontinue if tinnitus develops.
- Pregnant women should avoid willow bark in the third trimester (risk of bleeding complications and premature closure of the ductus arteriosus).
- Accurate dosing is difficult with raw bark because salicin content varies by tree, season, and preparation method. Start with low doses and increase gradually.

### Personal Protective Equipment

- Gloves for bark stripping (rough bark can abrade skin)
- No special PPE required for tea preparation

## Quality Control

### Acceptance Criteria

- **Dried bark**: Inner surface pale tan to light brown, slightly bitter and astringent taste, no mold, no wood adhering
- **Bark powder**: Light brown, finely ground, distinctive bitter taste

### Testing Methods

- Taste test: salicin has a characteristic bitter taste. Bitter bark = higher salicin content.
- Water extract test: steep 1 g of bark powder in 10 ml of hot water. Add ferric chloride solution. A violet color indicates salicylate presence.
- Particle size: powder should pass through a coarse sieve (1-2 mm mesh) for even extraction.

## Scaling Notes

- **Individual tree**: 10-50 g of dried bark per harvest from a 3-year-old coppice shoot. Enough for 5-25 therapeutic doses.
- **Coppice plot** (0.01 hectare): 20-40 stools. Annual bark yield: 200-1,000 g dried. 100-500 doses of pain relief per year.
- **Community planting** (0.1 hectare): 200-400 stools along waterways. Annual production sufficient for a village pharmacy.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|---------------|----------|
| Bark tastes bland, not bitter | Wrong species or harvested at wrong time | Confirm white willow identification; harvest in spring |
| Tea causes stomach upset | Too concentrated or taken on empty stomach | Reduce dose; take with food; try cold infusion instead of hot |
| Bark difficult to peel from shoots | Harvested outside of spring sap flow | Harvest in spring when bark separates easily |
| No pain relief effect | Bark too old or stored improperly | Use fresh bark or recently dried bark; salicin degrades over time |
| Willow cuttings fail to root | Soil too dry or cuttings planted upside up | Keep soil consistently moist; plant right-side up (bud orientation) |

## Variations and Alternatives

- **Purple willow** (*Salix purpurea*): Higher salicin content (up to 3% in bark). Smaller tree but preferred for medicine.
- **Crack willow** (*Salix fragilis*): Common in Europe, moderate salicin content. Large tree.
- **Aspirin (synthetic)**: Acetylsalicylic acid synthesized from phenol. First produced in 1897. More consistent dosing, gentler on the stomach. Requires chemical industry.
- **Meadowsweet** (*Filipendula ulmaria*): Herb containing salicylate compounds. Gentler than willow bark. Used traditionally for the same purposes.
- **Wintergreen** (*Gaultheria procumbens*): Contains methyl salicylate, a potent topical anti-inflammatory. Applied as an oil to sore muscles and joints.
- **Poplar bark** (*Populus spp.*): Contains salicin, similar to willow. Less concentrated but useful where willow is unavailable.

### Willow in Land Reclamation

Willow is one of the best plants for stabilizing eroding riverbanks and reclaiming degraded land.
The dense, fibrous root system binds soil effectively, and the rapid growth provides quick canopy cover.
Live willow stakes (2-3 year old shoots, 1-2 meters long) can be driven directly into wet ground where
they will root and grow, establishing vegetation cover within a single growing season.

For riverbank stabilization, plant willow in a crisscross pattern (fascine planting): lay bundles of
dormant willow shoots in shallow trenches along the contour of the bank and peg them in place. The
shoots root at each node, creating a living retaining wall that strengthens as it grows. This technique,
used since Roman times, provides permanent erosion control without imported materials.

Willow also serves as a biofuel crop in short-rotation coppice systems. Harvesting coppiced willow
every 3-4 years produces 10-15 tonnes of dry biomass per hectare per year, suitable for firewood or
charcoal production. The rapid regrowth from the cut stump makes this one of the most productive
woody biomass systems available in temperate climates.

Willow roots readily from cuttings — a freshly cut willow stick pushed into moist ground will sprout roots and leaves within weeks. This property makes willow ideal for live fencing, erosion control, and rapid establishment of windbreaks. The Salix genus contains over 400 species distributed across all temperate regions, ensuring that a suitable willow species is available in virtually any temperate location. Willow biomass is also being investigated as a feedstock for bioethanol production due to its rapid growth on marginal land.
