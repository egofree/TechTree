# Gears & Gear Manufacturing

> **Node ID**: machine-tools.gears
> **Domain**: [Machine Tools](./index.md)
> **Dependencies**: None
> **Enables**: None
> **Critical**: Yes — gears are essential for every machine tool drive, transmission, and mechanism
> **Timeline**: Years 3-15
> **Outputs**: gears, gear_assemblies

Gears transmit rotary motion and torque between shafts through meshing teeth. Every machine tool spindle, lathe leadscrew drive, milling table feed, and hoist mechanism depends on gears. Without gear manufacturing capability, rotational power stays locked to a single shaft — no speed reduction, no directional change, no torque multiplication.

This document covers five gear types: spur (parallel shafts, simplest to produce), helical (parallel shafts, smoother mesh), bevel (intersecting shafts), worm (perpendicular shafts, high reduction ratio), and rack-and-pinion (rotary-to-linear conversion). Each type requires a lathe and milling machine from [Machining](./machining.md) and steel or cast iron stock from [Iron & Steel](../metals/iron-steel.md). Heat treatment of gear teeth uses the same carburizing and quenching processes described in [Metal Forming](./forming.md).

## Overview

A gear is a cylinder or cone with teeth cut into its periphery at a precise spacing called the circular pitch. When two gears mesh, the teeth of one fit into the gaps of the other, forcing both to rotate together. The gear ratio — the ratio of tooth counts — determines how speed and torque change between the input and output shafts. A 20-tooth pinion driving a 60-tooth gear reduces output speed to one-third and triples output torque.

Gear manufacturing adds two requirements beyond basic [Machining](./machining.md): involute tooth profiles (the specific curve shape that gives smooth, constant-velocity mesh) and indexable dividing (the ability to space teeth equally around the blank). The involute profile is generated by the cutting tool geometry and relative motion during cutting, not by freehand shaping. Equal spacing requires a dividing head or index plate — a rotary fixture that locks the workpiece at precise angular intervals.

The material choices for gears follow the load requirements. Light-duty gears (hand cranks, instrument drives) use brass or bronze. Medium-duty gears (lathe feed drives, mill table feeds) use cast iron or mild steel. Heavy-duty gears (mine hoists, rolling mill drives) use case-hardened steel — a low-carbon steel core with a high-carbon surface layer hardened by carburizing and quenching. See [Iron & Steel](../metals/iron-steel.md) for steel grades and heat treatment.

## Process Description

### Spur Gears

**Principle**: Spur gears have straight teeth parallel to the axis of rotation. Two spur gears on parallel shafts mesh to transmit motion. The involute tooth profile ensures the line of action remains constant, producing smooth power transfer at a fixed gear ratio. Module (m) = pitch diameter / number of teeth. For metric gears, all dimensions derive from module: addendum = m, dedendum = 1.25 × m, tooth thickness = π × m / 2.

**Prerequisites**:
- [Lathe](./iterative-bootstrap.md) for turning gear blanks to pitch diameter + 2 × addendum
- [Milling machine](./machining.md) with dividing head for indexing teeth
- Involute gear cutter matched to module and tooth count range, or single-point form tool

**Materials**:
- [Mild steel bar stock](../metals/iron-steel.md), 55 mm diameter × 25 mm length (for 50 mm OD, module 2, 24-tooth gear)
- [Soluble oil emulsion](../chemistry/lubricants.md), flood coolant
- Involute gear cutter, module 2, cutter #5 (for 21-25 teeth range)

**Procedure** (cutting a 24-tooth spur gear, module 2, 50 mm OD):

1. Turn the blank on the lathe. Face both sides to 20 mm thickness (±0.05 mm parallelism). Turn the OD to 50.00 mm (±0.02 mm). Bore the center hole to the required shaft diameter (e.g., 15.00 mm +0.01/-0.00 mm for a press fit). Chamfer both corners 1 mm × 45°.
2. Mount the dividing head on the milling machine table. Align the dividing head spindle parallel to the milling machine spindle within 0.02 mm over 100 mm using a dial indicator on a test bar.
3. Mount the gear blank on an arbor in the dividing head. Use a spacer between the blank and the dividing head to allow full depth cutting. Tighten the arbor nut.
4. Mount the involute gear cutter (module 2, #5 for 21-25 teeth) on the milling machine arbor. The cutter width must clear the blank face.
5. Calculate the indexing: 24 teeth from a 40:1 dividing head requires 40/24 = 1 full turn + 16/24 of a turn. Select the 24-hole circle on the index plate. Set the sector arms to span 16 holes.
6. Set spindle speed: RPM = (1000 × 25) / (π × 50) = 159 RPM for mild steel with HSS cutter at 25 m/min. Set table feed: 20-40 mm/min (manual feed preferred for gear cutting to control chip load).
7. Position the cutter centered on the blank face. Touch the cutter to the blank OD (feeler gauge, 0.05 mm). Set the vertical dial to zero.
8. Raise the knee (or lower the spindle) by the full depth of cut: 2.25 × module = 2.25 × 2 = 4.5 mm. Engage the cutter and feed across the blank face. Apply flood coolant.
9. After the first tooth space is cut, retract the knee, index the dividing head by one division (1 full turn + 16 holes on the 24-hole circle), and cut the next tooth space.
10. Repeat for all 24 teeth. Measure tooth thickness at the pitch circle with a gear tooth vernier: target 3.14 mm (π × m / 2). Adjust depth if tooth thickness is oversize — recut all teeth at increased depth.

**Expected performance**: Tooth spacing accuracy: ±0.05 mm on pitch. Tooth thickness tolerance: ±0.03 mm. Surface finish: 3-6 μm Ra on tooth flanks. Cutting time: 3-5 minutes per tooth, 72-120 minutes for a 24-tooth gear. Tool life: 50-100 teeth per cutter sharpening in mild steel.

**Applications**: Lathe change gears, milling machine feed drives, hand-crank mechanisms, clockwork mechanisms, conveyor drives, pump drives.

**Strengths**:
- Simplest gear type to design and manufacture — straight teeth, no axial thrust
- Full range of cutting methods available: form milling, hobbing, shaping
- Easy to measure and inspect with basic tools (vernier caliper, gear tooth gauge)
- No axial thrust load — simple bearing arrangements suffice

**Weaknesses**:
- Noisy at high speed — teeth engage and disengage abruptly, causing vibration
- Limited to parallel-shaft applications only
- Single-tooth contact at any instant limits load capacity compared to helical gears
- Requires exact module cutter for each tooth range (8 cutters per module for form milling)

### Helical Gears

**Principle**: Helical gears have teeth cut at an angle (helix angle, typically 15-30°) to the axis. The angled teeth engage gradually — contact starts at one end of the tooth and sweeps across — producing smoother, quieter operation than spur gears. The helix angle generates an axial thrust force that must be carried by a thrust bearing. Module calculations use the normal module (mn = m × cos(helix angle)).

**Prerequisites**:
- [Lathe](./iterative-bootstrap.md) for turning blanks
- [Milling machine](./machining.md) with dividing head and universal table (swiveling to helix angle)
- Helical milling cutter or hand-ground form tool at the helix angle

**Materials**:
- [Mild steel or alloy steel bar stock](../metals/iron-steel.md), diameter = OD / cos(helix angle) + margin
- [Soluble oil emulsion](../chemistry/lubricants.md), flood coolant
- Involute gear cutter matched to normal module

**Procedure** (cutting a 24-tooth helical gear, module 2, 20° helix angle):

1. Turn the blank on the lathe to the required OD: for a 20° helix angle, the blank OD = spur OD / cos(20°) = 50 / 0.940 = 53.2 mm. Face to 20 mm thickness. Bore center hole.
2. Mount the dividing head on the mill table. Swivel the milling machine table (or tilt the dividing head) to the helix angle (20°). Verify the angle with a protractor within ±0.5°.
3. Set up the dividing head with change gears connecting it to the table feed screw. The gear train rotates the dividing head as the table feeds, producing the helical path. Calculate the lead: L = π × pitch diameter / tan(helix angle). For a 48 mm pitch diameter at 20°: L = π × 48 / tan(20°) = 415 mm.
4. Mount the involute cutter (normal module 2). Position the cutter at the helix angle.
5. Calculate indexing for 24 teeth. Set the dividing head index plates.
6. Cut tooth spaces one at a time, feeding the table longitudinally while the dividing head rotates. Each tooth space requires one pass at full depth (4.5 mm for module 2).
7. Index and repeat for all 24 teeth.

**Expected performance**: Tooth spacing accuracy: ±0.05 mm. Surface finish: 2-5 μm Ra (smoother than spur due to sweeping engagement). Helix angle accuracy: ±0.5°. Axial thrust force: F_axial = F_tangential × tan(helix angle) — for 20° helix, thrust ≈ 36% of tangential load.

**Applications**: Automotive transmissions, machine tool gearboxes, industrial reducers, any application requiring quiet, high-speed operation.

**Strengths**:
- Smoother, quieter operation than spur gears — gradual tooth engagement distributes load
- Higher load capacity — multiple teeth share the load simultaneously
- Can connect parallel shafts or crossed shafts (with opposite hand helix angles)

**Weaknesses**:
- Axial thrust force requires thrust bearings — increases bearing complexity and cost
- More complex to manufacture — requires helical milling setup or hobbing machine
- Helix angle errors cause uneven load distribution and noise
- Harder to measure tooth geometry — requires special gauges

### Bevel Gears

**Principle**: Bevel gears transmit motion between intersecting shafts (typically 90°). Teeth are cut on the conical surface of a frustum. The pitch cone angle for each gear equals the shaft angle × (tooth count of gear / total tooth count). Straight bevel gears have teeth radiating from the cone apex; spiral bevel gears have curved teeth for smoother engagement.

**Prerequisites**:
- [Lathe](./iterative-bootstrap.md) for turning conical blanks
- [Milling machine](./machining.md) with dividing head set to the pitch cone angle
- Bevel gear cutter (or single-point tool ground to the bevel tooth profile)

**Materials**:
- [Steel or cast iron bar stock](../metals/iron-steel.md) — blanks are turned to a frustum (truncated cone)
- [Soluble oil emulsion](../chemistry/lubricants.md), flood coolant
- Bevel gear cutter set (roughing and finishing cutters for the specific cone angle)

**Procedure** (cutting a straight bevel gear pair, 90° shaft angle, 16/32 teeth, module 2):

1. Calculate pitch cone angles: pinion angle = arctan(16/32) = 26.6°, gear angle = arctan(32/16) = 63.4°. Cone distance = pitch diameter / (2 × sin(cone angle)) = 32 / (2 × sin(26.6°)) = 35.8 mm.
2. Turn the blanks on the lathe to frustum shape. Set the compound rest to the pitch cone angle. Turn the outer conical surface. Face the front and back faces. Bore the center hole.
3. Mount the dividing head on the mill table. Tilt the dividing head to the root cone angle (pitch cone angle minus addendum angle). For a 26.6° pitch cone angle with module 2: root cone angle ≈ 24.6°.
4. Mount a bevel gear cutter on the milling arbor. The cutter must be matched to the module and approximate tooth count.
5. Index for the required number of teeth (16 or 32). Cut tooth spaces by feeding the cutter into the tilted blank.
6. Bevel gear teeth are not uniform width — they taper from outer to inner end. Cut in two passes: a roughing pass leaving 0.5 mm on the tooth flanks, then a finishing pass to final dimension.
7. After cutting all teeth, check mesh with the mating gear. Adjust tooth contact pattern by modifying the mounting distance (shimming) — contact should be centered on the tooth face, biased slightly toward the toe (small end).

**Expected performance**: Tooth contact pattern: 50-60% of tooth face length centered. Backlash: 0.05-0.15 mm at the outer end. Cone angle accuracy: ±0.25°. Shaft angle tolerance: ±0.5°. Surface finish: 3-6 μm Ra on tooth flanks.

**Applications**: Hand drills, differential drives, right-angle gearboxes, mill head drives, vehicle differentials.

**Strengths**:
- Only practical method for transmitting motion between intersecting shafts
- Compact right-angle drive — no need for additional idler gears
- Straight bevel gears are simpler to manufacture than spiral bevels

**Weaknesses**:
- Tooth profile varies along the face width — requires specialized cutters or multi-pass setups
- Sensitive to mounting distance errors — 0.1 mm shift moves the contact pattern significantly
- Load capacity limited for straight bevels — one tooth pair in contact at a time
- Spiral bevels require dedicated machines (Gleason-type) — not feasible in early bootstrap

### Worm Gears

**Principle**: A worm gear set consists of a worm (a screw-like cylinder with one or more threads) meshing with a worm wheel (a gear with teeth cut to match the worm's thread profile). The worm drives the worm wheel, providing high reduction ratios in a single stage (10:1 to 60:1 typical). The worm wheel teeth are throated — concave to partially wrap around the worm. This increases the contact area and load capacity.

**Prerequisites**:
- [Lathe](./iterative-bootstrap.md) for turning the worm (a threaded shaft) and the worm wheel blank
- [Milling machine](./machining.md) with dividing head for gashing the worm wheel teeth
- HSS fly cutter or form tool matching the worm thread profile

**Materials**:
- [Steel rod](../metals/iron-steel.md) for the worm (EN8 / AISI 1045, 25-40 mm diameter)
- [Phosphor bronze or cast iron](../metals/iron-steel.md) for the worm wheel (bronze preferred for low friction)
- [Cutting oil](../chemistry/lubricants.md) (sulfurized mineral oil)
- HSS fly cutter ground to match the worm thread profile

**Procedure** (cutting a single-start worm and 40-tooth worm wheel, module 3):

1. **Worm**: Turn the worm blank on the lathe to the required diameter: worm OD = (q × module) + 2 × module, where q is the diameter quotient (typically 8-12). For q = 10, module 3: OD = (10 × 3) + (2 × 3) = 36 mm. Face both ends, bore centers.
2. Cut the worm thread on the lathe using the threading setup. For a single-start worm with module 3: pitch = π × 3 = 9.42 mm. Set change gears for this pitch. Use a tool ground to the thread profile (trapezoidal for worm threads). Multiple passes at increasing depth until full thread depth (2.25 × module = 6.75 mm radial depth) is reached.
3. **Worm wheel blank**: Turn to the required throat diameter: worm wheel throat diameter = (number of teeth × module) + 2 × module = (40 × 3) + (2 × 3) = 126 mm. Turn the throat radius on the lathe using a form tool — the rim is concave with a radius matching the worm OD / 2 = 18 mm. Face and bore center hole.
4. Mount the worm wheel blank on the dividing head. Tilt the dividing head so the blank axis is perpendicular to the milling spindle (worm wheel teeth are cut tangentially).
5. Gash the teeth: use a cutter matched to the worm thread space width. Set the dividing head to index 40 teeth. Cut each tooth space to the full depth at the throat.
6. Finish the tooth flanks with a fly cutter ground to the worm thread profile. Rotate the worm wheel by hand while the fly cutter engages, simulating the meshing action. This "hobbing" action generates the correct involute-throated profile.
7. Check mesh: mount the worm and worm wheel in a test fixture. Check that the worm rotates freely and the wheel advances by exactly one tooth per worm revolution. Measure backlash at the wheel teeth: target 0.05-0.15 mm.

**Expected performance**: Reduction ratio: 40:1 (single start, 40 teeth). Efficiency: 40-70% for single-start worm (friction losses are significant). Backlash: 0.05-0.15 mm. Load capacity: limited by the bronze wheel material — typical rating 0.5-5 kW at 100-500 RPM worm speed. Self-locking: single-start worms with lead angle <5° are self-locking (cannot be back-driven).

**Applications**: Winches, hoists, elevator drives, valve actuators, indexing mechanisms, tuning knobs (self-locking prevents back-drive).

**Strengths**:
- Highest single-stage reduction ratio — 40:1 to 60:1 in one compact pair
- Self-locking capability (when lead angle < friction angle) — the load cannot drive the worm backward
- Quiet operation — sliding contact produces less noise than spur or helical mesh
- Compact package for right-angle drive with high reduction

**Weaknesses**:
- Low efficiency (40-70%) — significant friction losses generate heat, require lubrication
- Worm wheel requires bronze or similar low-friction material — steel-on-steel wears rapidly
- Throated wheel profile is complex to cut accurately — requires fly cutter hobbing setup
- Heat generation limits continuous duty — requires oil bath or forced lubrication for sustained operation

### Rack-and-Pinion

**Principle**: A rack is a straight bar with teeth cut along one edge; a pinion is a spur gear that meshes with the rack. The arrangement converts rotary motion (pinion) to linear motion (rack) or vice versa. Linear displacement per pinion revolution = π × module × number of pinion teeth. Rack teeth are straight-sided (involute profile with infinite pitch radius).

**Prerequisites**:
- [Lathe](./iterative-bootstrap.md) for turning the pinion blank
- [Milling machine](./machining.md) for cutting rack teeth (table feeds the rack past the cutter) and pinion teeth (dividing head)
- Gear cutter matched to the module

**Materials**:
- [Steel flat bar](../metals/iron-steel.md) for rack (EN3 / AISI 1020, 20 × 20 mm cross-section, length as required)
- [Steel round bar](../metals/iron-steel.md) for pinion (EN3 / AISI 1020, 25-50 mm diameter)
- [Soluble oil emulsion](../chemistry/lubricants.md), flood coolant
- Involute gear cutter (module 2) for pinion teeth, rack cutter for rack teeth

**Procedure** (cutting a module 2 rack and 20-tooth pinion):

1. **Pinion**: Turn the blank on the lathe to 44 mm OD (20 teeth × module 2 + 2 × module 2). Face to 20 mm width. Bore center hole to shaft size. Cut teeth on the milling machine with dividing head (same procedure as spur gear, section above).
2. **Rack**: Mill the steel flat bar to 20 × 20 mm cross-section. Face one edge flat (this becomes the tooth reference surface). Check flatness with a straightedge: <0.05 mm over 500 mm.
3. Mount the rack on the mill table with the tooth edge facing up. Clamp with strap clamps every 100 mm. Align the rack lengthwise with the table travel using a dial indicator: <0.02 mm over the full length.
4. Calculate the linear pitch: π × module = π × 2 = 6.28 mm. This is the distance between adjacent teeth on the rack.
5. Mount a rack milling cutter (or a standard involute cutter if the rack is wide enough) on the arbor. Set cutter height: touch the cutter to the rack reference surface, then raise by the full tooth depth (2.25 × module = 4.5 mm).
6. Cut the first tooth space. Use the table longitudinal feed dial to index to the next tooth position: advance by 6.28 mm. Cut the next tooth space.
7. Repeat for all teeth. For a 500 mm rack with module 2: 500 / 6.28 = 79.6 teeth (79 full teeth).
8. Check tooth spacing with a vernier caliper: measure across 10 teeth — must be 62.83 mm ±0.05 mm.

**Expected performance**: Linear positioning accuracy: ±0.05 mm per pinion revolution. Tooth spacing accuracy on rack: ±0.03 mm. Backlash: 0.05-0.10 mm. Load capacity: determined by tooth bending strength — module 2 rack in mild steel rated for ~500 N tangential force at 10 mm tooth face width.

**Applications**: Lathe carriage drive (rack-and-pinion feed), drill press quill feed, steering mechanisms, machine tool slides, CNC linear axes (with anti-backlash nuts), elevator doors.

**Strengths**:
- Converts rotary to linear motion directly — no leadscrew or ball screw needed
- Rack can be made to any length by joining sections — not limited by lathe bed length
- Simple tooth geometry — rack teeth are straight-sided, easier to cut than involute curves
- High stiffness in the drive direction — no leadscrew wind-up

**Weaknesses**:
- Backlash between pinion and rack reduces positioning accuracy — requires preload mechanism for precision applications
- Rack alignment over long lengths is difficult — cumulative tooth spacing errors cause binding
- Open tooth mesh is exposed to contamination — requires covers in dirty environments
- Limited to lower loads than leadscrew drives of comparable size — tooth bending strength vs. screw thread shear strength

## Scaling Notes

**Single gear (workshop)**: One machinist with a lathe, mill, and dividing head produces 2-4 gears per day (module 2-3, 20-40 teeth). Bottleneck is tooth cutting time — each tooth requires indexing and a milling pass. A dividing head with quick-index capability doubles throughput.

**Small batch (10-50 gears)**: Set up the dividing head and cutter once, then cut identical gears in sequence. Batch cutting reduces setup time per gear from 60 minutes to 10 minutes. Tooth cutting time per gear remains 60-120 minutes (unchanged). Total: 8-16 gears per day.

**Hobbing (production scale)**: A hobbing machine uses a worm-shaped cutter (hob) that meshes with the gear blank, generating all teeth simultaneously in a continuous feed. A single hob cuts any gear of a given module regardless of tooth count. Throughput: 10-50 gears per hour depending on module and material. Requires a specialized hobbing machine — built from a lathe with a synchronized hob spindle, or purchased as a dedicated machine. See [EDM, CNC & Precision Grinding](./edm-cnc.md) for CNC hobbing.

**Minimum economic scale**: A single involute gear cutter (one of 8 per module) costs 2-4 hours to make from HSS stock and produces 50-100 gears before resharpening. Making the cutter set (8 cutters) is justified when producing 20+ gears of a given module.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|---------------|----------|
| Uneven tooth spacing (alternating thick and thin teeth) | Dividing head error — sector arms set to wrong hole count, or indexing pin not seating fully in hole | Verify sector arm calculation: holes spanned = (divisions per turn × work divisions) / required teeth. Clean index plate holes. Ensure pin seats fully before each cut |
| Tooth thickness oversize by >0.05 mm | Depth of cut insufficient — blank OD oversize or vertical dial zeroed incorrectly | Recut all teeth with increased depth. Measure tooth thickness after recut — remove half the excess per pass to avoid overshooting |
| Chatter marks on tooth flanks (wavy pattern 0.05-0.2 mm apart) | Cutter worn or setup lacks rigidity — arbor deflection, dividing head backlash, or excessive feed rate | Resharpen or replace cutter. Reduce feed rate to 20 mm/min. Support arbor with overarm bracket. Check dividing head lock before each cut |
| Gear mesh too tight (teeth bind, requires force to rotate) | Center distance too short, or tooth thickness oversize | Increase center distance by shimming. If tooth thickness is at nominal, the center distance is wrong. If teeth are oversize, recut at increased depth |
| Excessive backlash (>0.2 mm at pitch circle) | Center distance too large, or tooth thickness undersize from overcutting | Reduce center distance. If teeth are undersize, the gear cannot be repaired — remake with shallower cut depth. For worn gears in service, machine a replacement pinion (smaller gear wears faster) |
| Helical gear has axial vibration | Thrust bearing absent or inadequate — helical axial force not absorbed | Install a thrust bearing (ball or tapered roller) on the shaft carrying the helical gear. Size for F_axial = F_tangential × tan(helix angle) |
| Worm wheel overheating (>80°C in 30 minutes of operation) | Insufficient lubrication or wrong material pairing (steel-on-steel) | Worm wheels must be bronze (PB1 or PB2) or cast iron — never steel. Provide oil bath lubrication covering the worm-wheel mesh. Check oil level and viscosity (ISO VG 220 worm gear oil) |
| Bevel gear contact pattern biased to toe or heel | Mounting distance incorrect — gears not positioned at their design cone distance | Adjust shimming to move the gear along its axis. Contact biased to toe (small end) means mounting distance too large — reduce shimming. Contact biased to heel means distance too small — increase shimming |

## Safety

- **Eye protection mandatory**: Gear milling produces sharp, hot steel chips ejected at high speed. Safety glasses (ANSI Z87.1 rated) at minimum. Face shield for gear hobbing operations where chip volume is high. A broken HSS cutter fragment at 159 RPM carries enough energy to penetrate an eyeball.
- **Pinch points on dividing head**: The dividing head spindle and index crank create pinch points. Keep fingers clear of the spindle area during indexing. Lock the dividing head before cutting — a spinning blank with exposed teeth is a severe pinch hazard.
- **Burn hazards from heat treatment**: Carburizing at 900-950°C and quenching in oil produces oil mist that can ignite. Quench in a ventilated area with a fire blanket and Class B extinguisher within reach. Quench oil temperature must stay below 80°C — hot oil breaks down and releases acrid fumes. Use tongs 400+ mm long for handling hot gears. Leather gloves rated for 300°C contact heat.
- **Abrasive wheel hazards during gear grinding**: If grinding gear teeth for finishing, ring-test the grinding wheel before mounting (suspend and tap — clear ring = sound, dull thud = cracked). Never exceed rated wheel speed. Guard covers 180° of wheel periphery. Grinding dust from steel contains respirable particles (<10 μm) — use dust extraction or wet grinding.
- **Entanglement risk during hobbing**: The rotating hob and gear blank create a severe entanglement hazard. No loose clothing, gloves, rings, or long sleeves near the hobbing machine. Emergency stop must be reachable from the operator's position. See [Machining](./machining.md) safety section for general machine shop hazards.

## Quality Control

**Acceptance criteria for machined gears**:
- Tooth spacing: ±0.05 mm on pitch (measured with gear tooth vernier)
- Tooth thickness at pitch circle: ±0.03 mm (measured with gear tooth vernier caliper)
- Blank OD: ±0.02 mm (measured with micrometer)
- Bore diameter: +0.01/-0.00 mm for press fit, ±0.01 mm for sliding fit (measured with inside micrometer)
- Face width: ±0.05 mm (measured with vernier caliper)
- Runout (radial): <0.03 mm total indicated reading (measured with dial indicator on gear blank mounted between centers)
- Backlash at pitch circle: 0.05-0.15 mm for general-purpose gears

**Tooth contact pattern test**: Coat the teeth of one gear with a thin layer of Prussian blue marking compound (or engineers' blue). Mesh the gears and rotate through several revolutions by hand. The transferred blue on the mating teeth shows the contact pattern. Acceptable contact: 50-60% of tooth face height centered on the pitch line. Concentrated contact at tooth tips or roots indicates profile errors — recut or replace.

**Pitch measurement**: Measure across n teeth with vernier calipers. The span measurement M = (n - 0.5) × circular pitch + tooth thickness. For module 2, 24 teeth, measuring across 3 teeth: M = 2.5 × 6.28 + 3.14 = 18.84 mm ±0.05 mm.

**Hardened gear verification**: After case hardening, check tooth surface hardness with a file test (hardened surface resists a new file) or a Rockwell hardness tester (target: 55-62 HRC on tooth surface, 25-35 HRC core). Check for distortion by remeasuring bore diameter and runout — quenching can warp thin-walled gears.

## Variations and Alternatives

**Gear cutting methods compared**:

| Method | Equipment Needed | Accuracy | Throughput | Best For |
|--------|-----------------|----------|------------|----------|
| Form milling (involute cutter) | Mill + dividing head + cutter set | ±0.05 mm | 2-4 gears/day | Small batches, workshop production |
| Single-point form tool | Lathe + dividing head + ground tool | ±0.08 mm | 1-2 gears/day | One-off gears, no cutter available |
| Hobbing | Hobbing machine + hob | ±0.02 mm | 10-50 gears/hour | Production volumes, any tooth count |
| Gear shaping | Shaping machine + cutter | ±0.03 mm | 5-20 gears/hour | Internal gears, cluster gears |
| Casting (no machining) | Foundry + pattern | ±0.5 mm | 20+ gears/hour | Low-speed, low-load applications |
| 3D printing (polymer) | FDM/SLA printer | ±0.2 mm | 1-4 gears/hour | Prototyping, low-load instruments |

**Material alternatives**:
- **Bronze gears** (phosphor bronze PB1): Used for worm wheels and low-speed meshes running against steel. Self-lubricating, quiet, resistant to galling. Cannot match steel's load capacity.
- **Nylon gears** (cast or machined nylon 6/6): Used for light loads, instrument drives, food-processing machinery (no lubrication needed). Low noise. Limited temperature range (80°C max continuous).
- **Cast iron gears** (GG20-25): Used for low-speed, medium-load applications. Good wear resistance, low cost, easy to machine. No heat treatment required. Not suitable for high-speed or shock-load applications.

**Gear profile alternatives**:
- **Cycloidal teeth**: Used in clockwork and instruments. Smoother at very low speeds but more sensitive to center distance errors. Rarely used for power transmission.
- **Stepped gears** (two thin spur gears offset by half a tooth pitch): Poor man's helical gear — reduces noise without the helical thrust load. Requires careful assembly to maintain the half-tooth offset.
