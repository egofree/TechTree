## Overview

Black alder (*Alnus glutinosa*) is a deciduous tree native to Europe that produces timber with a remarkable property: it is highly resistant to decay when permanently submerged in water or buried in wet ground. Alder piles driven into riverbeds and marshes can last for centuries. Venice was built on alder pilings, and many medieval structures in northern Europe rest on alder foundations.

The wood is relatively soft and light (450-550 kg/m³ at 12% moisture content) but becomes extremely hard and durable when permanently wet. This paradox (soft when dry, durable when wet) makes alder uniquely suited for foundation pilings, underwater structures, sluice gates, and any construction that contacts water or damp ground.

Alder is also a nitrogen-fixing tree, hosting Frankia bacteria in root nodules that convert atmospheric nitrogen to plant-available forms. A stand of alder adds 50-150 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year to the soil. This makes it valuable for reclaiming degraded land, improving soil before planting other crops, and stabilizing stream banks.

The bark contains 8-16% tannins, usable for leather tanning though inferior to oak or wattle bark. The bark was also used historically to produce red-brown and black dyes.

The tree grows 10-25 meters tall, thrives in wet soils and along waterways, and reaches harvestable size in 15-25 years. Alder regrows vigorously from cut stumps (coppices), providing a sustained yield of poles and timber on a 10-15 year cycle.

Primary outputs: `water_resistant_timber` for underwater construction, `nitrogen_fixation` for soil improvement, `tannin_bark` for leather and dye.

## Prerequisites

### Materials

- Alder seeds or bare-root seedlings
- Wet or moist site: riverbank, marsh edge, or poorly drained soil
- Clay, loam, or peat soils (tolerates waterlogging better than most trees)

### Equipment

- Felling axe, crosscut saw, or chainsaw
- Driving cap (heavy metal ring) and maul for pile driving
- Drawknife for bark stripping
- Basic hand tools for woodworking

### Knowledge

- Alder identification (round leaves with notched tips, cone-like fruit)
- Pile driving technique for foundation work
- Coppice management for sustained production
- Nitrogen fixation benefits for crop rotation planning

### Infrastructure

- Wet site planting area
- Pile driving equipment for construction use
- Coppice woodland management plan

## Process Description

### Timber Production

1. Plant seedlings or direct-sow seeds in moist to wet soil. Space 2-3 meters apart for timber, 1-1.5 meters for coppice.
2. Allow trees to grow for 15-25 years for structural timber, or coppice on 10-15 year cycles for poles.
3. Fell during winter when sap is down. Alder fells and works more easily than oak or ash.
4. For underwater use: cut trunks into piles of required length (3-10 meters). Remove bark (optional, as bark also resists decay underwater). Shape the base to a point for driving.
5. Drive piles into the riverbed or marsh using a drop hammer or maul. The pile should penetrate below the water table into firm substrate.
6. Once driven, the pile is permanently submerged and will resist decay for 100+ years.

### Nitrogen Fixation for Soil Improvement

1. Plant alder on degraded, nitrogen-poor soils. The tree grows where few other species survive.
2. Allow 5-10 years of growth. During this period, the root nodules fix 50-150 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year.
3. Clear the alder (harvest timber and fuel) and plant crops in the nitrogen-enriched soil. The nitrogen is released as the alder roots and leaf litter decompose.
4. Alternatively, interplant alder with other trees in a mixed woodland to maintain soil fertility.

## Safety Considerations

- Pile driving is hazardous. Heavy piles and driving equipment can cause crushing injuries. Use mechanical assistance. Never stand under a raised pile.
- Alder wood dust can cause respiratory irritation. Wear a mask when sawing or sanding.
- Working in wet conditions (typical for alder harvest) increases risks of slips, falls, and hypothermia. Wear appropriate footwear and clothing.
- Felling trees near waterways creates falling hazards into water. Plan felling direction carefully.

### Personal Protective Equipment

- Steel-toed waterproof boots for wet-site work
- Hard hat for pile driving and felling
- Safety glasses for sawing and woodworking
- Dust mask for sanding operations

## Quality Control

### Acceptance Criteria

- **Foundation piles**: Straight, minimum 15 cm diameter at butt, bark removed, base pointed for driving, no heartshake or large knots
- **Coppice poles**: Straight, minimum 5 cm diameter, no rot, even taper
- **Bark for tanning**: Inner surface orange-brown, no rot, no wood adhering

### Testing Methods

- Pile quality: tap the butt end with a hammer. A clear ring indicates sound wood. A dull thud indicates internal decay.
- Water resistance verification: cut a cross-section and soak in water for 30 days. Sound alder shows no softening or decay.
- Nitrogen fixation: dig up roots and check for visible nodules (small, coral-like growths on roots). Active nodules are pink inside.

## Scaling Notes

- **Individual tree**: Produces one pile (3-10 m long) or several poles.
- **Riparian strip** (1 hectare): 2,000-5,000 trees. Annual timber yield: 10-30 cubic meters from coppice rotation. Nitrogen input: 50-150 kg per year.
- **Foundation project**: A modest building on wet ground requires 50-200 piles, each 3-6 meters long. This requires 50-200 mature alder trees.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|---------------|----------|
| Piles decay after driving | Not driven below permanent water table | Drive deeper; ensure pile is always submerged |
| Alder grows poorly on dry site | Species adapted to wet conditions; drought stress | Plant in moist or wet locations; irrigate during establishment |
| Coppice shoots die back | Fungal infection (Phytophthora) or frost damage | Improve drainage; select frost-resistant individuals |
| Wood warps badly during drying | Rapid, uneven drying | Stack with close stickers; weight the stack; dry slowly |

## Variations and Alternatives

- **Red alder** (*Alnus rubra*): North American species with similar properties. Grows faster in the Pacific Northwest.
- **Italian alder** (*Alnus cordata*): Tolerates drier conditions. Good for land reclamation in Mediterranean climates.
- **Elm** (*Ulmus minor*): Also water-resistant and used for underwater structures, though elm is heavier and harder to work.
- **Oak** (*Quercus robur*): Heartwood is durable in ground contact but less so than alder when fully submerged. Much stronger but slower growing.
