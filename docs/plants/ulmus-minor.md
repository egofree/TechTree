## Overview

Field elm (*Ulmus minor*) is a deciduous tree native to Europe that produces timber with a distinctive combination of properties: it is resistant to decay when alternately wet and dry, it does not split easily, and it has interlocking grain that makes it exceptionally resistant to splitting. These properties made elm the traditional choice for wagon wheel hubs, water pipes, coffin boards, and any application where wood must withstand wetting and drying cycles without cracking.

Elm wood has a density of 540-680 kg/m³ at 12% moisture content, with a characteristic interlocked grain that makes it nearly impossible to split with wedges. This same interlocked grain makes elm difficult to work with hand tools but gives it remarkable toughness and resistance to splitting under impact. A wagon wheel hub made from elm could withstand years of pounding from iron tires and stone roads.

The tree grows 20-30 meters tall with a spreading, vase-shaped crown. It is fast-growing in its early years, reaching harvestable size in 40-60 years. Elm tolerates a wide range of soils, including heavy clay, and withstands urban pollution better than most trees. It propagates readily from root suckers.

Elm has been devastated by Dutch elm disease (caused by the fungus *Ophiostoma novo-ulmi*, spread by elm bark beetles) across Europe and North America since the 1920s. Most mature elm trees in Europe have been killed. However, the roots survive and produce suckers that grow to 5-10 meters before succumbing to the disease. Disease-resistant cultivars are being developed.

For a civilization bootstrapping context, elm's key value is in wheel-making and water management. Wagon wheels are fundamental to transport, and the wheel hub is the most stressed component. Elm's resistance to splitting makes it uniquely suited for this role.

Primary outputs: `tough_timber` for wheel hubs, water pipes, and applications requiring split resistance.

## Prerequisites

### Materials

- Elm seeds (samaras, wind-dispersed in spring) or root suckers
- Well-drained soil (tolerates heavy clay)
- Temperate climate

### Equipment

- Felling and sawing equipment (elm is difficult to saw due to interlocked grain)
- Lathe or turning tools for wheel hub manufacture
- Boring tools for axle holes
- Adze and chisels for shaping

### Knowledge

- Elm identification (rough bark, asymmetric leaf base)
- Wood properties: interlocked grain and its implications for working
- Wheel hub manufacture: turning, boring, and fitting
- Dutch elm disease management and resistant variety selection

### Infrastructure

- Elm woodland or plantation
- Wheelwright workshop with lathe (if available)
- Access to trees of adequate size (40+ cm diameter for wheel hubs)

## Process Description

### Timber Production

1. Plant from seed or suckers. Elm grows rapidly in youth. Space 5-8 meters apart for timber.
2. Allow 40-60 years for timber-quality trees. The trunk must reach 40+ cm diameter for wheel hub blanks.
3. Fell in winter. Elm is prone to rapid staining if processed in warm weather. Saw within 2 weeks of felling.
4. Saw or rive (split) into billets. The interlocked grain makes splitting difficult; sawing is preferred despite rapid blade dulling.
5. Air dry in a covered stack. Elm seasons moderately quickly: 25 mm boards need 4-8 months; large hub blanks (150 mm) need 2-3 years.

### Wheel Hub Manufacture

1. Select a straight-grained elm billet at least 150 mm in diameter and 200 mm long. The grain must run along the axis of the hub.
2. Turn the billet on a lathe to a smooth cylinder, slightly larger than the finished hub diameter.
3. Bore the central axle hole through the hub using a spoon auger or lathe boring. The hole must be precisely centered and sized to fit the axle with minimal play.
4. Cut mortises (slots) around the circumference for the spokes. Typically 12-16 mortises evenly spaced. Each mortise must be angled to match the spoke dishing.
5. Shape the exterior: the hub is slightly barrel-shaped (wider in the middle, narrower at the ends). Turn the finished shape on the lathe.
6. The elm hub is assembled with spokes (typically ash) and felloes (rim sections, typically oak or ash). An iron tire is heated, fitted over the wheel, and cooled to shrink it tight.

## Safety Considerations

- Elm dust can cause respiratory irritation and skin sensitization. Wear a dust mask and gloves when sanding or machining.
- The interlocked grain causes unpredictable kickback when sawing elm with power tools. Use sharp blades, slow feed rates, and stand clear of the blade path.
- Elm logs are heavy and awkward. Use mechanical assistance for lifting and moving.
- Dutch elm disease creates standing dead trees with brittle branches. Do not work under dead elms.

### Personal Protective Equipment

- N95 dust mask for all machining operations
- Safety glasses for sawing and turning
- Leather gloves for handling rough timber
- Hard hat in elm woodland with dead trees

## Quality Control

### Acceptance Criteria

- **Wheel hub blanks**: Straight grain running the full length, no knots, no heartshake, minimum 150 mm diameter
- **Structural timber**: No major shake, knot size under 30 mm, grain slope less than 1:8

### Testing Methods

- Split test: try to split a small sample with a wedge. Good elm resists splitting. If it splits easily, it is not suitable for hubs.
- Grain interlock check: examine the end-grain. The fibers should cross at angles, visible as alternating light and dark bands.
- Moisture content: weigh-dry-weigh method. Target 12-18% for wheel hubs.

## Scaling Notes

- **Individual tree**: 1-3 cubic meters of sawn timber, or 10-30 wheel hub blanks from a well-formed trunk.
- **Woodland management**: Plant disease-resistant cultivars in mixed-species woodland. Elm should be 10-20% of the mix, not a monoculture.
- **Bottleneck**: The 40-60 year rotation and Dutch elm disease. Elm is a long-term, strategic timber resource.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|---------------|----------|
| Hub cracks during use | Hidden defect, or grain not aligned with axle | Select only straight-grained blanks; inspect carefully before turning |
| Saw blade dulls rapidly on elm | Interlocked grain and abrasive silica | Use carbide-tipped blades; reduce feed rate |
| Timber stains brown after felling | Delayed processing in warm weather | Process within 2 weeks; store logs in water if delay is unavoidable |
| Dutch elm disease killing trees | Fungal infection via bark beetles | Plant resistant cultivars; remove infected trees promptly; avoid pruning in beetle season |
| Hub bore is off-center | Inaccurate boring technique | Use a lathe for boring; mark center clearly before starting |

## Variations and Alternatives

- **Wych elm** (*Ulmus glabra*): Similar timber properties, native to mountainous regions of Europe. Better cold tolerance.
- **American elm** (*Ulmus americana*): Similar properties, also devastated by Dutch elm disease.
- **White ash** (*Fraxinus americana*): Tough, shock-resistant timber. Good alternative for wheel hubs, though less split-resistant than elm.
- **Oak** (*Quercus robur*): Stronger and more durable than elm but splits more readily. Can be used for hubs if grain selection is careful.
