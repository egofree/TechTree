# Curing and Finishing

> **Node ID**: transport.tire-manufacturing.curing-and-finishing
> **Domain**: [Transport](./index.md)
> **Dependencies**: [`transport.tire-manufacturing`](tire-manufacturing.md)
> **Timeline**: Years 20-40+
> **Outputs**: solid_tires
> **Critical**: No

Curing (vulcanization) converts the soft, plastic green tire into an elastic, dimensionally stable finished product. The green tire is placed in a **curing press** between two heated metal mold halves. A flexible rubber bladder (the curing bag) inflates inside the tire with high-pressure steam or hot water (1.5-2.5 MPa), pressing the tire outward against the engraved mold sidewalls while the mold itself is heated to 150-180 C. Sulfur cross-links the polymer chains over 8-20 minutes; the tread pattern and sidewall lettering are molded in during the same step. See [Polymers](../polymers/index.md) for the vulcanization reaction chemistry.

## Post-Cure Operations

- **Post-cure inflation** — passenger and light-truck tires are mounted immediately on a rim and inflated to operating pressure while still hot, holding the carcass dimensionally stable as cross-linking completes over the first hours. Prevents post-cure growth and shape distortion.
- **Trimming** — flash and vents (rubber forced into mold vent holes during cure) are trimmed off by hand or rotating knives.
- **Balancing** — static and dynamic balance measured on a spin balancer; adhesive or clip-on lead/steel weights applied to the rim flange. An unbalanced tire causes vibration at speed.
- **Uniformity inspection** — radial and lateral force variation measured under load against a test wheel. High-force-variation tires are downgraded or ground-corrected (a light skim cut to true the geometry).

## Quality Control

Every tire is visually inspected and a sample is X-rayed (steel belt placement, voids) or shearographed (holographic separation detection). Final grading stamps the speed rating, load index, and DOT date code onto the sidewall. Tires older than ~10 years from the date code are retired regardless of tread depth — the rubber oxidizes and hardens past the point of safe grip.
