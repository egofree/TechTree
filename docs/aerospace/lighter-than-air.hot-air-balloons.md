# Hot-Air Balloons

> **Node ID**: aerospace.lighter-than-air.hot-air-balloons
> **Domain**: [Aerospace](./index.md)
> **Dependencies**: [`aerospace.lighter-than-air`](lighter-than-air.md)

> **Outputs**: balloons
> **Critical**: No

The hot-air balloon is the simplest manned flying machine ever built. A fabric envelope traps heated air (typically 100-120°C above ambient), producing lift through density difference. A propane burner suspended below the envelope mouth provides continuous heat. The pilot controls altitude by varying burner output — adding heat to ascend, allowing the envelope to cool to descend. Lateral navigation exploits wind currents at different altitudes: ascending into a wind layer moving in the desired direction, then descending past it.

## Envelope Design

A typical sport balloon envelope holds 1,500-3,000 m³ of hot air, constructed from 20-30 fabric gores (vertical panels) sewn together with load-bearing tapes. The fabric is high-tenacity ripstop nylon or polyester weighing 60-90 g/m², coated with polyurethane or silicone for reduced porosity and UV resistance. The envelope mouth (bottom opening) is reinforced with Nomex or other flame-resistant fabric to withstand direct burner impingement. A parachute valve at the top crown allows controlled hot-air release for descent.

## Burner and Fuel System

Modern balloon burners use liquid propane vaporized in a coil above the flame, producing a powerful, clean-burning jet. A typical system produces 2-5 MW of heat output. Fuel is carried in aluminum or stainless-steel cylinders (20-40 liters each), with 2-4 cylinders providing 45-90 minutes of sustained flight. A pilot light maintains a small flame at all times; the main blast valve is opened in short bursts (5-15 seconds) to add heat.
