## Overview

Paper mulberry (*Broussonetia papyrifera*) is a fast-growing deciduous tree native to East Asia, cultivated for millennia for its inner bark fiber. The bark produces strong, flexible sheets that are beaten into tapa cloth (bark cloth), the primary textile of many Pacific Island cultures. The same bark fiber is the raw material for some of the finest handmade papers in China and Japan, including the durable paper used for woodblock printing, calligraphy, and documents that last centuries.

The tree grows 10-15 meters tall with a spreading crown. It is dioecious (separate male and female trees) and spreads aggressively by root suckers, forming dense thickets if not managed. The bark is the primary product: the inner bark (phloem) contains long, strong fibers 5-12 mm in length that bond naturally when beaten together on a flat surface.

Paper mulberry grows in a wide range of climates, from tropical to temperate (hardy to -15°C). It tolerates poor soils and disturbed sites, making it a pioneer species that colonizes cleared land. The tree coppices vigorously, producing new shoots from the cut stump. For bark production, trees are managed as coppiced shrubs harvested on a 1-3 year cycle, producing straight, branch-free shoots 2-4 meters tall.

In East Asia, the bark fiber is processed into high-quality paper through a vat method: the bark is cooked in alkali (wood ash lye), beaten into a pulp, suspended in water, and formed into sheets on a screen. The resulting paper is strong, flexible, and long-lasting. In the Pacific, the bark is beaten directly into sheets of tapa cloth without pulping.

Primary outputs: `bark_fiber` for tapa cloth and papermaking, `wood` for fuel.

## Process Description

### Bark Harvesting

1. Coppice (cut) established trees at ground level during the dormant season (winter in temperate zones). The stump produces multiple new shoots in the following growing season.
2. After 1-3 years of growth, harvest shoots when they reach 2-4 meters tall and 2-4 cm diameter at the base. The bark strips most easily in spring when sap is flowing.
3. Cut the shoot at ground level and remove branches. Make a lengthwise slit through the bark from top to bottom.
4. Peel the bark from the wood in one continuous strip. Grasp the bark at the top of the slit and pull downward. A single shoot yields a bark strip 2-4 meters long and 10-20 cm wide.
5. For tapa cloth: soak the bark strip in water for 12-24 hours to soften it. For papermaking: dry the bark for storage or process immediately.

### Tapa Cloth Production

1. Soak fresh bark strips in water for 12-24 hours until pliable.
2. Place the soaked bark strip, inner surface up, on a wooden anvil. The anvil should be a flat, smooth log or stone slab.
3. Beat the bark with a wooden mallet, working from one end to the other. The beating spreads and thins the fibers, creating a wider, thinner sheet. Turn the bark over periodically and beat both sides.
4. As the sheet thins and widens, overlap the edges of adjacent sheets and beat the overlap to fuse the fibers together. This creates larger sheets than a single bark strip could produce.
5. Continue beating until the sheet reaches the desired thinness (0.5-2.0 mm). Fine tapa is thin, flexible, and nearly translucent.
6. Dry the finished tapa cloth flat in the sun. It can be painted, dyed, or printed with traditional patterns.

### Papermaking (East Asian Method)

1. Strip bark from harvested shoots. Remove the outer dark bark by scraping, leaving only the pale inner bark (phloem).
2. Cook the inner bark in an alkali solution (wood ash lye or lime) at 80-100°C for 2-4 hours. The alkali breaks down the non-fibrous material and separates the fibers.
3. Rinse the cooked bark thoroughly in running water to remove the alkali and dissolved impurities.
4. Beat the rinsed bark with a wooden mallet on a flat surface until it separates into individual fibers and forms a pulp. Alternatively, grind in a stone mortar.
5. Suspend the pulp in a vat of water (approximately 1% pulp by weight). Stir to distribute the fibers evenly.
6. Dip a papermaking mold (frame with fine mesh screen) into the vat and lift it out horizontally, allowing a thin layer of fibers to settle on the screen. The water drains through the mesh, leaving a wet sheet of paper.
7. Couch (transfer) the wet sheet onto a flat board or felt. Stack sheets with interleaving cloths.
8. Press the stack to remove excess water. Peel individual sheets and dry them on smooth boards or heated surfaces.

### Bark Processing Details

The quality of paper mulberry fiber depends on careful separation of inner bark (phloem) from outer bark (dead periderm). The outer bark contains dark pigments and shorter, weaker fibers that discolor and weaken the final product. Scraping must remove all traces of the dark outer layer while preserving the maximum thickness of the pale inner bark.

For the finest Japanese washi paper (made from the closely related *Broussonetia kazinoki*), the inner bark is steamed rather than boiled, then stripped in long ribbons. These ribbons are dried in the sun for storage and can be reconstituted months later by soaking in water overnight. The dried bark strips are a stable trade commodity — lightweight, compact, and shelf-stable for years.

Alkali cooking breaks down the middle lamella (the pectin-rich layer between fibers) and allows the fibers to separate during beating. The traditional alkali source is wood ash lye (potassium carbonate solution, pH 11-12) or lime (calcium hydroxide suspension). Soda ash (sodium carbonate) is more effective if available. Over-cooking weakens the fibers; under-cooking leaves them bonded and difficult to separate. Test for doneness by pulling the cooked bark: properly cooked bark separates into fibrous strands with gentle pulling.

### Paper Sheet Formation

The vat method of sheet formation produces the most uniform paper. The key variable is pulp concentration: 0.5-1.5% pulp in water for thin writing papers, 2-3% for heavier printing or wrapping papers. Japanese papermakers add a mucilage from tororo-aoi (sunset hibiscus root) to the vat, which suspends fibers evenly and prevents clumping. Without this additive, stir the vat continuously and form sheets quickly before fibers settle.

Each dip of the mold produces one sheet. The wet sheet is couched (transferred) onto a flat board or felt, building a stack called a post. The post is pressed under heavy weights or in a screw press to remove 60-70% of the water. Individual sheets are then peeled from the interleaving cloths and dried on smooth boards, heated metal plates, or in the sun. Drying time: 4-12 hours depending on temperature and humidity.

## Safety Considerations

- The milky sap of paper mulberry is a skin irritant and can cause allergic contact dermatitis in sensitive individuals. Wear gloves when stripping fresh bark.
- Wood ash lye (alkali solution used in papermaking) is caustic and causes chemical burns on skin and eyes. Wear gloves and eye protection when handling lye.
- Beating bark for tapa cloth is repetitive work that can cause wrist and arm strain. Take breaks and alternate arms.
- Paper mulberry pollen is highly allergenic. Male trees produce large quantities of airborne pollen. Plant female trees only in populated areas.
- The cooked bark pulp is hot. Allow it to cool before handling, or wear heat-resistant gloves.

### Personal Protective Equipment

- Gloves for bark stripping and lye handling
- Eye protection when working with alkali solutions
- Dust mask when beating dry bark or handling dried pulp
- Heat-resistant gloves for hot pulp handling

## Quality Control

### Acceptance Criteria

- **Inner bark for paper**: Pale cream to white, free of outer bark fragments, flexible
- **Paper sheets**: Uniform thickness, no holes or thin spots, strong enough to handle without tearing when damp
- **Tapa cloth**: Uniform thickness, no holes, fibers well-fused at overlaps, flexible

### Testing Methods

- Fold test for paper: fold a sheet in half, then in half again. Good paper folds without cracking along the crease.
- Tear test: tear a strip of paper. Long, fibrous tear indicates strong fiber bonding. Short, clean tear indicates weak bonding.
- Light transmission test: hold paper up to light. Evenly translucent = uniform thickness. Dark or light spots indicate thick or thin areas.
- Tapa cloth stretch test: gently pull the cloth in both directions. Good tapa stretches slightly without tearing.

## Scaling Notes

- **Individual coppice stool**: Produces 5-15 shoots every 2 years, yielding 2-5 kg of green bark per harvest.
- **Small plantation** (0.5 hectare): 1,000-2,000 coppice stools on a 2-year rotation. Annual bark yield: 1-3 tonnes. Sufficient for a village-level paper or tapa operation.
- **Papermaking workshop**: One person can produce 50-100 sheets of paper per day using traditional methods. A team of 3-4 (cooking, beating, forming, drying) produces 200-400 sheets per day.
- **Bottleneck**: Beating bark to pulp is labor-intensive. Water-powered trip hammers (used in traditional Chinese papermaking) reduce labor by 80%.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|---------------|----------|
| Bark does not peel from wood | Harvested outside of spring sap flow | Harvest in spring; soak shoots in water for 24 hours before peeling |
| Paper sheets have holes | Pulp too coarse or screen damaged | Beat pulp longer to finer consistency; repair or replace screen |
| Paper is weak and tears easily | Insufficient cooking in alkali | Extend cooking time; check alkali concentration |
| Tapa cloth cracks at overlaps | Insufficient beating at the overlap zone | Beat overlap areas more thoroughly to fuse fibers |
| Inner bark dark brown instead of cream | Outer bark not fully removed | Scrape more thoroughly to remove all dark outer bark |
| Coppice shoots diseased or stunted | Fungal infection or poor drainage | Remove diseased shoots; improve drainage; space stools farther apart |

## Variations and Alternatives

- **Mulberry** (*Morus alba*): Related species grown primarily for silkworm fodder. The bark is also used for paper but is inferior to paper mulberry.
- **True mulberry paper** (Kozo): Made from *Broussonetia kazinoki*, a close relative. Produces the finest Japanese handmade paper (washi).
- **Hemp paper**: Hemp produces excellent paper fiber and yields more per hectare. Requires retting to extract fiber, unlike bark-based papermaking.
- **Linen rag paper**: Recycled linen textiles produce the highest-quality Western paper. Requires textile production first.
- **Papyrus** (*Cyperus papyrus*): Egyptian writing material made by laminating sliced pith strips. Not true paper but serves a similar purpose.
- **Bamboo paper**: Bamboo fiber produces serviceable paper but requires more aggressive processing (longer cooking, harder beating) than mulberry bark.
- **Cotton rag paper**: Recycled cotton textiles produce high-quality paper. Requires textile production infrastructure first.

### Non-Paper Bark Products

Beyond paper and tapa cloth, paper mulberry bark produces cordage with moderate strength. Strip the inner bark into narrow ribbons, soften by chewing or soaking, and twist or braid into rope. The resulting cordage is flexible and moderately strong but degrades faster than hemp or sisal cordage when exposed to moisture. The bark also yields a fiber suitable for weaving into rough matting or padding when processed by retting (soaking in water for 5-10 days to separate fibers from the bark matrix).

### Tapa Cloth in Pacific Cultures

Tapa cloth (bark cloth) was the primary textile of Polynesian and Melanesian societies,
used for clothing, bedding, ceremonial regalia, and wrapping sacred objects. The cloth is
made by beating paper mulberry bark directly into sheets without the pulping step used in
papermaking. The resulting fabric is soft, flexible, and can be sewn or glued (with starch
paste) into larger pieces.

Tapa production is a communal activity in many Pacific cultures. The bark is harvested,
soaked, and beaten by women working in groups, with the rhythmic sound of beaters serving
as a social activity. The finished cloth is painted with traditional patterns using natural
dyes (brown from koka bark, red from mati bark, black from soot). Each island group has
distinctive patterns that identify the wearer's social rank and island of origin.

For civilization bootstrapping, tapa cloth provides the simplest textile production method
available, requiring no spinning, weaving, or complex tools. A flat surface, a mallet, and
fresh bark are sufficient to produce serviceable fabric.
