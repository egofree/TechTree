## Overview

Shaping and joining wood into structures, furniture, patterns, and tool handles using hand saws, planes, chisels, and measuring tools. Carpentry provides wooden patterns for metal casting, structural framing for buildings, and finished goods. The fundamental wood-working capability that supports construction and manufacturing.

This technology is characteristic of the Stone Age era of industrial development. It builds on earlier foundational techniques while enabling more precise and controlled manufacturing outcomes.

Primary outputs: `wooden-structures`, `furniture`, `wooden-patterns`. These materials or products serve as inputs for downstream manufacturing and processing steps.

The craft develops in direct relationship with available tools. Stone blades can split and scrape green wood; bronze adds durability to edge tools; iron saws and plane blades enable precise dimensioning; steel edges hold sharpness long enough for production work. Each metallurgical advance improves what a carpenter can achieve, but the fundamental operations — measuring, marking, cutting to a line, and joining pieces — remain constant across technology levels.

Wood is anisotropic: its properties differ along the three principal axes (longitudinal, radial, tangential). Shrinkage on drying is roughly 0.1% longitudinally, 2-5% radially, and 5-10% tangentially. This differential shrinkage is the source of warping, checking, and joint loosening — and the reason seasoned lumber is preferred for precise work.

## Prerequisites

### Materials

- **Wood** — primary structural material. Species selection depends on application:
  - **Oak**: Dense, hard, resistant to rot. Structural beams, furniture, boat frames. Difficult to work with hand tools but extremely durable.
  - **Pine/Fir**: Soft, straight-grained, easy to work. Framing lumber, shelving, general construction. Widely available in temperate regions.
  - **Ash**: Flexible, tough. Tool handles, bent components, wagon parts. Excellent shock resistance.
  - **Maple**: Hard, dense, fine-grained. Wear surfaces, workbench tops, pattern-making. Takes precise cuts well.
  - **Basswood/Linden**: Soft, even texture, minimal grain. Carving, pattern-making for metal casting. The traditional choice for foundry patterns.
- **Fasteners** — iron nails, wooden pegs (trunnels), iron screws (when available), animal-hide glue, casein glue
- **Abrasives** — sandstone, pumice, shark skin, or crushed garnet for progressive smoothing

### Equipment

- **Saws**: Crosscut saw (teeth filed at an angle for cutting across grain), rip saw (teeth filed straight for cutting with grain), coping saw (thin blade for curves and interior cuts). A well-sharpened crosscut saw leaves a cleaner surface than a dull one regardless of technique.
- **Planes**: Jack plane (roughing, 355-455 mm sole), try plane (flattening, 508-610 mm sole), smoothing plane (finishing, 178-254 mm sole), block plane (end-grain and small work). Blade sharpness determines surface quality more than any other factor.
- **Chisels**: Bench chisels (general purpose, 6-50 mm widths), mortise chisels (thick, heavy, for deep cuts), firmer chisels (stronger, for heavier work). Keep edges honed — a dull chisel requires excessive force and slips.
- **Boring tools**: Brace and bit (auger bits 6-25 mm, expansive bits for larger holes), hand drill (smaller holes, gimlet bits). Hand-powered, adequate for all furniture and pattern work.
- **Measuring and marking**: Folding rule or tape, try square (90-degree reference), sliding bevel (angle transfer), marking gauge (scribes parallel lines), story pole (records repeated dimensions without measurement).
- **Striking tools**: Wooden mallet for chisel work (does not damage chisel handles), claw hammer for nail driving and removal. Mallet weight matched to chisel size — a heavy mallet on a small chisel causes loss of control.
- **Clamping**: Bar clamps, C-clamps, and wooden wedges for assembly. Clamp pressure must be sufficient to close joints but not so great as to deform the workpiece. Pad clamp faces to prevent marring finished surfaces.

### Knowledge

- Understanding of wood grain direction and its effect on cutting, planing, and joint strength
- Familiarity with wood moisture behavior — green vs. seasoned, equilibrium moisture content, seasonal movement
- Ability to read and transfer dimensions from drawings to workpieces
- Tool sharpening technique — edge geometry, honing angle, and stropping
- Safety training for cutting tool handling, heavy lifting, and fire prevention with combustible waste

### Infrastructure

- Covered workspace with good natural or artificial lighting — precision work requires clear visibility of grain, scribe marks, and joint surfaces
- Solid, flat workbench at waist height, 2-3 meters long, with vise(s) for holding workpieces. The bench must not rock or flex under planing pressure.
- Timber storage area — covered, well-ventilated, with level supports for stickered lumber stacks
- Sharpening station — bench stones (coarse and fine) or a grinding wheel, with a leather strop for final honing. Sharp tools are both safer and more accurate.
- Waste handling area for sawdust, shavings, and offcuts — separate flammable waste from the workspace

## Process Description

Carpentry transforms rough-sawn timber into finished structural components, furniture, and patterns through a sequence of measuring, marking, cutting, shaping, and joining operations. The craft demands understanding of wood grain direction, moisture behavior, and joint geometry. Green (unseasoned) wood works more easily but shrinks and warps as it dries; seasoned (dried) wood holds dimensions but resists cutting tools.

### Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Select timber appropriate to the intended product. Hardwoods (oak, ash, maple) for structural load-bearing and pattern work; softwoods (pine, fir, spruce) for framing and general construction. Inspect for grain direction, knots, shakes, and checks.
2. Measure and mark workpieces using a folding rule or story pole. Transfer dimensions from drawings or patterns. Square all reference edges with a try square before marking cuts.
3. Rough-cut to length with a crosscut saw (across grain) or rip saw (with grain). Cut slightly oversize — 2-3 mm — to allow for final dressing.
4. Surface and dimension with hand planes. Start with a jack plane (remove bulk), progress to a try plane (flatten), finish with a smoothing plane (surface quality). Work with the grain wherever possible; against-grain planing causes tear-out.
5. Cut joints — mortise and tenon, dovetail, half-lap, dado, or butt joints depending on load requirements and available skill. Fit joints dry before applying adhesive.
6. Assemble and secure with wooden pegs, nails, screws, or adhesive (hide glue from animal collagen, or casein glue from milk protein). Clamp joints under pressure and allow adhesive to cure fully before handling.
7. Apply finish — sand progressively through grits, then apply linseed oil, beeswax, or paint as the application requires.

### Joinery Selection Guide

| Joint Type | Strength | Application | Difficulty |
|------------|----------|-------------|------------|
| Mortise and tenon | High — resists racking | Table legs, frame construction | Moderate |
| Dovetail | Very high — resists pulling apart | Drawer fronts, box corners | High |
| Half-lap | Moderate — good for frameworks | Timber framing, cross-members | Low |
| Dado (housing) | Moderate — supports shelves | Shelving, cabinet carcases | Low |
| Butt joint + fasteners | Low to moderate | Rough framing, temporary work | Very low |

### Process Parameters

| Parameter | Range | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| Moisture content (green wood) | 30-100% | Easy to cut, prone to warping on drying |
| Moisture content (seasoned) | 8-18% | Stable dimensions, harder to work |
| Plane blade angle | 25-30 degrees | Lower angle for softwoods, higher for hardwoods |
| Mortise depth | 1.5× tenon width minimum | Deeper for structural joints |
| Adhesive curing time | 4-24 hours | Varies by adhesive type and temperature |

## Safety Considerations

This process involves specific hazards requiring trained personnel and protective measures:

- **Cuts and lacerations**: Saw teeth and chisel edges cause deep cuts. Keep tools sharp — dull tools require more force and are harder to control, increasing cut risk.
- **Splinter injuries**: Wood splinters can embed in skin and cause infection. Pull splinters promptly and clean the wound.
- **Eye damage**: Flying chips from chisel work and sawing. Safety glasses mandatory for all cutting and striking operations.
- **Back injuries**: Lifting heavy beams and timber. Use leg lifting technique; get help for loads over 25 kg.
- **Kickback**: Timber pinched in a saw bind can release violently. Never stand directly behind the cutting line.
- **Saw dust inhalation**: Prolonged exposure to hardwood dust causes respiratory irritation and long-term nasal effects. Work in ventilated spaces; damp-sweep dust rather than dry-sweeping.

### Personal Protective Equipment

- Safety glasses or face shield — mandatory for all cutting, chiseling, and striking operations
- Leather work gloves for handling rough timber (remove for precision operations to maintain grip)
- Respiratory protection when sanding or working in enclosed, dusty spaces
- Hearing protection when using powered cutting tools
- Steel-toe boots with metatarsal protection for heavy timber handling
- Leather apron to protect against stray chisel cuts and adhesive spills

### Emergency Procedures

- Maintain first aid kit with splinter removal tools, wound closure strips, and antiseptic. Cuts from sharp tools are the most common injury.
- Know locations of emergency shutoffs for any powered equipment
- Establish evacuation routes and assembly points
- Train all personnel on fire suppression — sawdust and shavings are highly flammable. Keep sand and water buckets near work areas. Never accumulate more than one day's worth of shavings in the shop.

## Quality Control

### Acceptance Criteria

- **Wooden Structures**: Joints must be tight-fitting with no gaps visible at the interface. Load-bearing members must be free of checks, shakes, or decay. Dimensions within specified tolerances.
- **Furniture**: Surfaces smooth and free of tool marks. Joints flush with no protrusion or offset. Drawers and doors operate without binding. Finish uniform with no runs or bare spots.
- **Wooden Patterns**: Dimensional accuracy sufficient for casting tolerance requirements. Draft angles present on all vertical surfaces (1-3 degrees minimum) to allow pattern withdrawal from sand molds. Surface finish smooth enough to prevent sand adherence.

### Testing Methods

- **Dimensional inspection**: Check all critical dimensions with a folding rule, calipers, or story pole. Structural framing: verify square with a try square at all corners and diagonal measurements (equal diagonals = square).
- **Joint fit test**: Dry-fit all joints before adhesive application. A properly fitted mortise and tenon should hold position by friction alone; a dovetail should seat fully with moderate hand pressure or a single mallet tap.
- **Moisture content assessment**: Weigh a sample piece, dry it thoroughly in an oven, weigh again. Moisture content = (wet weight - dry weight) / dry weight. Target: 12-18% for structural work, 8-12% for furniture and pattern-making. Excess moisture causes shrinkage, warping, and joint failure after assembly.
- **Surface quality**: Visual inspection under raking light (light at a low angle across the surface) reveals tool marks, tear-out, and unevenness invisible under direct lighting. Run a fingernail across the surface — any catch indicates a defect requiring further planing or sanding.
- **Load testing (structural)**: Apply proof loads to beams and joists before installation. A deflection of less than span/360 under rated load indicates adequate stiffness. Check for audible creaking or visible cracking.

### Sampling Protocol

- Inspect first-article output against full specification. Verify all dimensions, joint fit, and surface quality on the first completed piece.
- Sample subsequent production at defined intervals. For structural work, inspect every beam; for furniture batches, check every fifth piece minimum.
- Record all measurements for trend analysis. Track dimensional drift across batches — if tenons are consistently tight at the start of a run but loose at the end, the chisel is dulling and needs resharpening.
- Reject and investigate any out-of-specification results. Common causes: blade wear, moisture shift in timber, measurement reference error.
- Seasonal adjustment: wood dimensions shift with ambient humidity. Measure and cut in conditions similar to the installation environment when possible. Allow 1-2 mm clearance for cross-grain components that may swell.

## Scaling Notes

Transitioning from bench-scale to production involves these considerations:

- **Bench scale (individual craftsperson)**: Hand tools — saws, planes, chisels, braces. One worker produces furniture, small structural elements, and patterns. Output limited to what one pair of hands can shape in a day. Suitable for custom work and prototyping.
- **Pilot scale (workshop)**: 3-5 workers with specialized roles (sawyer, joiner, finisher). Bench-mounted mortisers, powered lathes (treadle or water-driven). Standardized jigs for repeated joint cutting. Batch production of identical components for buildings or furniture sets.
- **Production scale (mill)**: Water-powered or steam-powered saws, planers, and moulders. 10-50 workers. Standardized dimensional lumber output in volume. Pattern shop attached to foundry operations producing casting patterns in quantity.
- **Scaling throughput**: The bottleneck at every scale is tool sharpening. A production shop needs a dedicated sharpening station and a schedule for blade rotation. A dull plane produces tear-out that requires rework, consuming more time than the original cut.

Key scaling challenges: tool sharpening throughput (dull tools are the primary production bottleneck), material handling logistics for heavy timber, and maintaining consistent joint quality across multiple workers. Standardized jigs and templates are the primary scaling mechanism — a jig that guides a saw or chisel to the same cut every time eliminates skill variation between workers.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|---------------|----------|
| Tear-out when planing | Working against grain direction or blade dull | Reverse workpiece direction; sharpen blade; take lighter cuts |
| Joints too loose | Over-cut mortise or undersized tenon | Shim with thin veneer strips; recut if gap exceeds 0.5 mm |
| Warped boards after cutting | Internal stress released by sawing | Rough-cut oversize, allow to rest 24-48 hours, then surface to final dimensions |
| Splitting when nailing near end | Nails too close to edge or too large | Pre-drill nail holes with a brad awl; use smaller nails or blunt the nail tip |
| Adhesive joint failure | Surface contaminated or insufficient clamp pressure | Clean mating surfaces; ensure full contact under clamp pressure; check adhesive shelf life |
| Dull tools requiring excessive force | Blade edge rolled or chipped | Hone on a sharpening stone (coarse then fine); strop to polish the edge |
| Uneven finish | Inconsistent sanding pressure or skipped grits | Sand progressively through grits (coarse → medium → fine); use a sanding block for flat surfaces |

## Variations and Alternatives

- **Green woodworking**: Work freshly felled timber while still wet. Easier to split and shape with drawknives and wedges. Suitable for chairs, baskets, and rustic framing. Components shrink as they dry, tightening joints in some cases (wedge-secured tenons). Requires understanding of differential shrinkage rates.
- **Steam bending**: Place wood in a steam box (sealed wooden box with steam inlet) until pliable, then bend around a form and hold until dry and set. Produces curved components without cutting across grain — stronger than sawn curves. Oak, ash, and beech bend well; softwoods bend poorly.
- **Pattern-making for casting**: A specialized branch of carpentry producing wooden patterns used to form molds in sand. Patterns require draft angles, core prints, and allowances for metal shrinkage. Pattern dimensions must exceed the finished casting by the shrinkage factor of the metal being cast. Basswood and pine are preferred for ease of shaping.

### Material Handling

Proper handling of input materials and products is essential for consistent results:

- Store rough-sawn timber stickered (spaced with thin sticks between layers) in a covered, ventilated area to allow air circulation and even drying. Direct sun and rapid drying cause checking and warping.
- Stack lumber flat on a level foundation to prevent bowing and twisting. Support at close intervals (every 600 mm) for long pieces.
- Use FIFO (first-in, first-out) inventory management for seasoned lumber. Older stock has more stable moisture content.
- Label all stock with species, date received, and moisture content at measurement time.
- Inspect materials before use — reject any that show signs of fungal decay (soft spots, discoloration), insect damage (bore holes, frass), or structural checks.
- Keep finished workpieces protected from moisture changes during assembly. Rapid humidity shifts cause joints to loosen before adhesive cures.
- Segregate waste streams: sawdust for compost or fuel, offcuts for kindling or small projects, unusable scraps for disposal.
