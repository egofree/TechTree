## Overview

The India rubber tree (*Ficus elastica*) is a large evergreen fig tree native to northeast India, Nepal, Bhutan, and Myanmar. It produces a milky latex that contains rubber (cis-1,4-polyisoprene), though at lower concentration and lower quality than the Para rubber tree (*Hevea brasiliensis*). Before Hevea was widely cultivated, *Ficus elastica* was an important regional rubber source in its native range.

The latex is tapped from the tree by cutting the bark, similar to Hevea, though the yield is much lower (approximately 0.5-2.0 kg of dry rubber per tree per year vs. 2-5 kg for Hevea). The rubber quality is also lower: *Ficus elastica* rubber has higher resin content and lower elasticity than Hevea rubber, making it less suitable for thin films and high-performance applications. It is, however, adequate for basic waterproofing, elastic bands, and molded goods.

The tree grows 15-30 meters tall in its native habitat, with a spreading canopy and aerial prop roots. It is widely cultivated as an ornamental houseplant in temperate regions, where it grows slowly in pots. In tropical and subtropical climates, it can be planted outdoors as a landscape tree.

*Ficus elastica* tolerates a wider range of conditions than Hevea: it grows in cooler temperatures (down to 5°C), lower humidity, and poorer soils. This makes it a potential rubber source in subtropical regions where Hevea struggles. The tree is also more resistant to the South American leaf blight that threatens Hevea plantations.

The main limitation is yield. A well-managed Hevea plantation produces 1,000-2,000 kg of rubber per hectare per year. A *Ficus elastica* plantation produces perhaps 200-500 kg per hectare per year. For subsistence-level rubber needs in areas without Hevea, this may be acceptable.

Primary outputs: `natural_rubber` for waterproofing and elastic materials.

## Prerequisites

### Materials

- Seeds or cuttings from mature trees
- Tropical or subtropical climate (minimum 5°C winter temperature)
- Well-drained soil

### Equipment

- Tapping knife for bark incision
- Collection cups (coconut shells, clay pots, or metal cups)
- Coagulation trays or sheets
- Rolling equipment for sheet rubber
- Smokehouse or drying area for finished rubber

### Knowledge

- Tapping technique: making bark cuts at the correct angle and depth
- Latex collection and preservation (ammonia or lime to prevent premature coagulation)
- Rubber sheet formation and drying
- Basic vulcanization if sulfur is available

### Infrastructure

- Plantation or individual trees at suitable spacing (5-8 meters apart)
- Tapping trails with collection cups
- Processing area for coagulation and sheet formation
- Drying or smoking area for finished rubber

## Process Description

### Tapping and Collection

1. Select trees at least 5 years old with a trunk diameter exceeding 15 cm at 1.5 meters height.
2. Make a sloping cut (tapping cut) in the bark at a 30° angle from left to right, about 1.5 meters above ground. Cut depth: 1-2 mm into the bark, reaching the latex vessels but not cutting into the wood.
3. Attach a collection cup below the lower end of the cut. The latex drips into the cup over 2-4 hours.
4. Collect latex from cups daily. Typical yield: 20-80 ml of latex per tree per tapping (about 15-30% dry rubber content).
5. Refresh the cut by removing a thin slice (1-2 mm) of bark on the same cut line at the next tapping. Move to a new panel when the cut reaches the ground.
6. Allow tapped bark to heal for 2-3 years before retapping the same panel.

### Rubber Processing

1. Strain collected latex through a coarse cloth to remove bark fragments and insects.
2. Dilute latex to approximately 15% dry rubber content with water.
3. Add a coagulant: formic acid (2-3 ml per liter of latex), acetic acid, or alum solution. Stir gently.
4. Allow the latex to coagulate into a solid mass (2-4 hours). The coagulum is a soft, white-to-cream solid.
5. Remove the coagulum and pass it through rollers to form thin sheets (2-3 mm thick), squeezing out excess water and serum.
6. Dry the rubber sheets in a smokehouse (at 40-60°C for 4-7 days) or air-dry in a well-ventilated area (2-3 weeks). Smoke-dried rubber resists mold better.
7. Store dried sheets in a cool, dry place away from direct sunlight.

## Safety Considerations

- *Ficus elastica* latex is a skin and eye irritant. The proteolytic enzymes in the latex can cause dermatitis. Wear gloves when handling fresh latex.
- Formic acid and acetic acid used for coagulation are corrosive. Dilute carefully and avoid skin contact.
- The smokehouse produces carbon monoxide and particulate smoke. Ensure ventilation. Do not enter an enclosed smokehouse while burning.
- *Ficus elastica* fruit (small figs) is not edible and can cause digestive upset if consumed.
- Tapping knives are sharp. Cut away from the body.

### Personal Protective Equipment

- Rubber gloves for latex handling
- Safety glasses for acid handling and latex work
- Leather gloves for smokehouse operations

## Quality Control

### Acceptance Criteria

- **Dried rubber sheets**: Uniform thickness (2-3 mm), pale brown color, no mold, no visible bark debris, elastic when stretched
- **Raw latex**: White to cream, free of debris, pH 6.0-7.0

### Testing Methods

- Stretch test: stretch a rubber strip to 300% length and release. Good rubber returns to within 10% of original length.
- Visual grading: pale, clean sheets are higher quality than dark or contaminated ones.
- Smell test: fresh rubber has a mild, slightly acrid smell. Strong ammonia or rotting smell indicates spoilage.

## Scaling Notes

- **Individual tree**: 0.5-2.0 kg dry rubber per year. A few trees provide enough rubber for household waterproofing needs.
- **Small plantation** (1 hectare): 200 trees. Annual rubber yield: 100-400 kg. Sufficient for community-level waterproofing and elastic materials.
- **Bottleneck**: Low yield per tree compared to Hevea. Not competitive for commercial rubber production but viable for subsistence in subtropical areas.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|---------------|----------|
| Latex does not flow from cut | Cut too shallow or tree dormant | Cut deeper (1-2 mm); avoid tapping during cold or dry periods |
| Latex coagulates in the cup | Latex left too long before collection; no preservative | Collect within 4 hours; add a few drops of ammonia to collection cups |
| Rubber sheets crack during drying | Drying too fast or uneven thickness | Dry more slowly; roll sheets to uniform thickness |
| Mold on drying sheets | High humidity, poor ventilation | Improve airflow; use smokehouse drying |
| Tapped bark shows disease | Fungal infection through wound | Clean tapping knife; avoid wounding wood beneath bark |

## Variations and Alternatives

- **Para rubber tree** (*Hevea brasiliensis*): Superior yield, quality, and processing characteristics. The world's primary natural rubber source. Tropical only.
- **Guayule** (*Parthenium argentatum*): Desert shrub rubber source. Different extraction method (whole-plant harvesting, not tapping). Arid zones.
- **Castilla elastica** (Panama rubber tree): Central American species with usable latex. Lower quality than Hevea but historically important in Mesoamerica.
- **Synthetic rubber**: Petroleum-based rubbers (SBR, neoprene, butyl) replace natural rubber for most applications at higher technology levels.
