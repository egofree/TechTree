## Overview

Agrimonia pilosa also known as hairy agrimony , is a flowering plant in the family Rosaceae . It is distributed primarily over the Korean Peninsula , Japan, China, Siberia , and Eastern Europe

Hairy agrimony has been a staple of East Asian herbal medicine for centuries, valued for its astringent, anti-inflammatory, and hemostatic properties. The plant's tannin-rich aerial parts are prepared as decoctions to treat digestive ailments, mouth and throat inflammation, and superficial wounds. Its yellow flower spikes also yield a pale yellow dye when processed with alum mordant.

Primary outputs: `medicinal_compounds` from dried aerial parts, with tannins, flavonoids, and triterpenoids as the key active constituents. The whole plant can also serve as a dye source producing yellow tones on textile fibers.

Hairy agrimony is a perennial herb growing 30 to 100 cm tall, with coarsely toothed compound leaves and small yellow flowers arranged in slender spikes. The plant grows along forest margins, hillsides, and grassy slopes, preferring moist but well-drained soils in temperate climates. It propagates by seed and by division of the root crown.

The aerial parts of the plant have a long history of use in traditional medicine across East Asia. Preparations include water decoctions, tinctures, and dried herb powder. The plant contains tannins, flavonoids, and triterpenoids that contribute to its astringent and anti-inflammatory properties. Traditional applications include treatment of digestive ailments, wound healing, and as a general tonic.

The plant's astringent action comes primarily from its tannin content, which ranges from 5 to 8 percent in dried aerial parts. These tannins bind to proteins in mucous membranes, creating a protective layer that reduces inflammation and fluid secretion. This mechanism underlies the herb's traditional use for diarrhea, sore throat, and wound care. The flavonoid quercetin and its derivatives contribute additional anti-inflammatory activity.

Agrimonia pilosa also produces a yellow dye from the whole plant when processed with an alum mordant. The dye produces a clear yellow on wool and silk, and a lighter yellow on cotton and linen. While not the strongest yellow dye available, the plant's dual medicinal and dye value makes it worth cultivating where both products are needed.

## Process Description

Agrimonia pilosa cultivation follows a straightforward perennial herb cycle: establish plants from seed or root division, maintain with moderate watering, and harvest aerial parts at early flowering when tannin content peaks. The harvested herb is shade-dried to preserve volatile compounds, then stripped and stored for use in decoctions and tinctures.

### Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Prepare planting site with loamy, slightly acidic soil amended with compost. Sow seeds in spring after frost danger passes, or plant root divisions in early spring or autumn.
2. Space plants 25 to 30 cm apart in rows 40 cm apart. Apply mulch to retain soil moisture and suppress weeds. Water during dry periods to maintain consistent soil moisture.
3. Allow plants to grow through the season. In the first year, limit harvest to allow root establishment. From the second year onward, harvest at early flower stage.
4. Cut stems 10 to 15 cm above ground level using clean scissors or a sickle. A second, lighter harvest may be possible in late summer if regrowth is vigorous.
5. Bundle harvested stems and hang upside down in a warm, well-ventilated area away from direct sunlight. Alternatively, spread stems on drying screens.
6. When fully dry (stems snap rather than bend), strip leaves and flowers from stems. Store in airtight glass jars or paper bags in a cool, dark location.

### Cultivation and Harvesting

Sow seeds in spring after frost danger passes, or start indoors and transplant after hardening off. Space plants 25 to 30 cm apart in rows 40 cm apart. Agrimonia tolerates a range of soil types but produces best in loamy, slightly acidic soil with consistent moisture. Apply a layer of mulch to retain soil moisture and suppress weeds.

The plant can also be propagated by dividing established root crowns in early spring or autumn. Each division should include several growing buds and a portion of the root system. Replant divisions immediately at the same depth as the parent plant.

Harvest aerial parts when the plant is in early flower, as this is when tannin and flavonoid concentrations peak. Cut stems 10 to 15 cm above ground level to allow regrowth. Use clean cutting tools to avoid introducing pathogens. A second, lighter harvest may be possible in late summer if the plant regrows vigorously.

### Processing and Storage

Bundle harvested stems and hang upside down in a warm, well-ventilated area away from direct sunlight. Alternatively, spread individual stems on drying screens. Turn the material daily to ensure even drying. The herb is fully dry when stems snap rather than bend.

Once dry, strip leaves and flowers from the stems by hand or by rubbing through a coarse screen. Store the dried herb in airtight glass jars or paper bags in a cool, dark location. Properly dried and stored herb retains potency for up to one year. For decoction preparation, crumble the dried herb and simmer in water for 15 to 20 minutes.

For tincture preparation, pack dried herb loosely into a glass jar and cover with 40 to 50 percent alcohol (vodka or diluted spirits). Steep for four to six weeks in a dark location, shaking gently every few days. Strain through cloth into amber glass bottles. The tincture has a longer shelf life than dried herb and concentrates the active compounds into a convenient liquid form. A typical traditional dose is 2 to 4 ml of tincture diluted in water, taken before meals for digestive complaints.

## Safety Considerations

Agrimonia is generally considered safe in traditional medicinal doses, but several hazards apply to cultivation and processing:

- Allergic reactions to plant compounds — skin contact with fresh plant can cause contact dermatitis in sensitive individuals
- Improper preparation toxicity — agrimony contains tannins that can cause nausea if consumed in excessive quantities over extended periods
- Tool injuries during harvesting — cuts from cutting tools
- Pest exposure — aphids and spider mites common on cultivated plants
- Soil-borne pathogens — root rot in poorly drained soils
- Dust inhalation when handling large quantities of dried herb

### Personal Protective Equipment

- Garden gloves for harvest and handling of fresh stems
- Dust mask when processing large quantities of dried herb to avoid respiratory irritation
- Long sleeves if sensitive to plant contact dermatitis
- Eye protection when cutting large bundles near face level
- Knee pads for extended low-level harvesting work

### Emergency Procedures

- For contact dermatitis: wash affected area with soap and water. Discontinue handling if rash develops.
- For dust inhalation: move to fresh air. If breathing difficulty persists, seek medical attention.
- For tool cuts: clean wound thoroughly, apply pressure to stop bleeding, and bandage. Seek medical attention for deep lacerations.
- Keep first aid supplies including bandages, antiseptic, and oral antihistamine for allergic reactions accessible in the drying and processing area.

## Quality Control

### Acceptance Criteria

- **Dried herb**: Green to yellow-green color, free of mold, foreign matter, and insect damage
- **Leaf material**: Intact leaves with visible glandular trichomes, indicating preserved volatile compounds
- **Decoction**: Amber-colored, distinctly astringent taste, free of particulate matter after straining

### Testing Methods

- Color and aroma assessment: properly dried herb should have a characteristic faint spicy aroma
- Moisture test: properly dried stems snap cleanly without bending
- Taste test: a small leaf should produce immediate astringency, confirming tannin presence
- Visual inspection for mold spots, insect damage, or foreign plant material contamination
- Rub test: dried leaves should crumble readily between the fingers without feeling leathery

### Sampling Protocol

For each harvest batch, test moisture content by weighing a sample before and after oven drying. Track year-over-year yield per plant to monitor crop health and soil fertility. Inspect each bundle for mold or insect damage before stripping. Record drying duration and conditions to correlate with potency retention.

## Scaling Notes

Hairy agrimony scales from garden plots to field plantings, though the herb's moderate yield per plant means production scale requires substantial planting area.

- **Bench scale**: Garden plot with 10 to 30 plants. Manual harvest and air drying. Individual herb bundles.
- **Pilot scale**: Field planting of 100 to 500 plants. Staggered planting for multiple harvests per season. Tens of kilograms of dried herb per year.
- **Production scale**: Multi-row field plantings of several thousand plants. Mechanized cultivation and semi-automated drying. Hundreds of kilograms per year.

Key scaling challenges: agrimony is slow to establish from seed, taking a full growing season before the first harvest. Post-harvest drying requires significant covered rack space proportional to harvest volume. Active compound concentration varies with soil conditions, harvest timing, and drying method, requiring consistent protocols to maintain product quality.

The plant's hooked bristles on the flower spikes serve as a natural seed dispersal mechanism, attaching to passing animals and dropping seeds along their travel routes. In cultivation, this means volunteer seedlings may appear in unexpected locations around the garden. These volunteers can be transplanted to dedicated beds or left in place as a self-sustaining expansion of the planting. The hooked bristles also make the plant easy to identify in the field, even when not in flower.

## Variations and Alternatives

- **Wild harvest**: Collecting from natural populations in suitable habitat. No cultivation required, but supply is seasonal and location-dependent.
- **Garden cultivation**: Controlled planting for reliable supply and consistent quality. Allows selection of vigorous plants for propagation.
- **Agrimonia eupatoria** (common agrimony): European species with similar medicinal properties. More widely documented in Western herbal traditions. Can be used interchangeably in most preparations.
- **Other astringent herbs**: Raspberry leaf, lady's mantle, and oak bark provide similar astringent properties for medicinal preparations where agrimony is unavailable.
- **Tincture preparation**: Steeping dried herb in alcohol preserves active compounds more effectively than water decoction and produces a shelf-stable product lasting several years.

### Companion Planting

Agrimony grows well alongside other Rosaceae family members and benefits from proximity to nitrogen-fixing plants. In mixed plantings, it attracts beneficial insects including hoverflies and parasitic wasps that help control pest populations on neighboring crops.

### Material Handling

Dried agrimony herb should be stored in opaque glass jars or paper bags in a cool, dark location. Exposure to light and humidity degrades active compounds. Label each container with harvest date and batch number. Inspect stored herb monthly for signs of mold or insect infestation. Spent plant material from decoction can be composted.

Agrimonia pilosa has been documented in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia and the Japanese Pharmacopoeia as an official medicinal herb. In traditional Korean medicine, the plant is used in combination with other astringent herbs for treating chronic diarrhea and intestinal inflammation. The cross-cultural consistency of these traditional applications across China, Korea, and Japan supports the plant's continued relevance as a medicinal herb, even as modern pharmaceutical alternatives become available.

The dried flower spikes are distinctive, with small hooked bristles that attach readily to clothing and animal fur, a seed dispersal mechanism that also serves as a useful identifying feature for wild-collected material.

In traditional Korean medicine, agrimony is prepared as a charred herb (geun-cho) by heating dried aerial parts in a dry pan until they blacken. This charring process is believed to enhance the herb's hemostatic properties, making it more effective for treating bleeding conditions. The charred herb is ground to a powder and taken with warm water. This processing variation is not used in Chinese or Japanese traditions and illustrates how different medical cultures develop distinct preparation methods for the same plant.

For dye extraction, harvest whole plants at flowering, chop coarsely, and simmer in water for one hour to extract the yellow pigment. Strain the liquid and add alum mordant (potassium aluminum sulfate) at 10 to 15 percent of fiber weight. Add pre-mordanted wool or silk to the dye bath and simmer for 45 minutes. Rinse dyed fiber in cool water and dry in shade. The resulting yellow is clear and lightfast, though not as intense as weld or dyer's broom yellow.

Agrimonia pilosa can be confused with Agrimonia eupatoria (common agrimony) in regions where both species grow. The two are distinguished by leaf shape and hair density: A. pilosa has more densely hairy stems and leaves, giving it a grayer appearance, and its leaflets are more deeply toothed. For medicinal purposes, the two species are generally used interchangeably, but accurate identification matters for propagation, as the species have slightly different growing requirements and medicinal compound profiles.

The dried herb is also incorporated into herbal bath preparations for treating skin conditions and promoting wound healing. A strong decoction added to bathwater provides a mild astringent effect on the skin. This topical application is considered one of the safest ways to use agrimony, as it avoids the potential gastrointestinal effects of internal consumption while still delivering the herb's tannin-based anti-inflammatory action to affected skin areas.
