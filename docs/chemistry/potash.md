## Overview

Production of potassium carbonate (potash) from wood ashes, mineral deposits, or brine evaporation. Potash is a fundamental alkali for glassmaking (potash glass), soap production, fertilizer, and chemical synthesis. Pearl ash (purified potash) was historically the primary source of alkali before synthetic soda ash.

The name tells the process: potash is literally "pot ash," the potassium-rich salts extracted by leaching fireplace ashes with water and boiling the solution down in iron pots. Before the discovery of underground potash deposits in the 1860s, this was the only route to alkali for glass, soap, and ceramics. A single cord of hardwood (about 3.6 m³) yields roughly 2-3 kg of crude potash, so the industry consumed forests wholesale. North American and Baltic forests fed European demand for decades.

Primary outputs: `potash`, `pearl-ash`, `alkaline-solutions`. Potash (K₂CO₃) goes into glass batches and soap kettles. Pearl ash is the recrystallized, purified form suitable for fine chemicals and optical glass. Alkaline solutions serve as leaching agents and pH adjusters across dozens of downstream processes, from textile processing to metal surface preparation.

Modern production mines sylvite (KCl) from underground evaporite deposits formed by ancient sea evaporation. These ores contain mixed sylvite and halite that flotation or solution mining separates. The wood ash route remains the fallback for any civilization without access to evaporite geology.

Potassium functions differently from the other two primary plant macronutrients. Nitrogen builds proteins and phosphorus builds energy transfer molecules (ATP), but potassium operates as an osmotic regulator and enzyme activator. It controls stomatal opening (and therefore water use efficiency in crops), activates starch synthase, and maintains cell turgor pressure. Potassium-deficient plants show symptoms of water stress even when soil moisture is adequate because they cannot regulate water movement between cells effectively. This makes potash fertilizer indispensable for achieving high crop yields, especially in intensive agriculture.

For glassmaking, potash glass (K₂CO₃ as the alkali source) has a lower melting point and greater brilliance than soda-lime glass (Na₂CO₃), but it is softer and less durable. Bohemian crystal, prized for its optical clarity and decorative potential, is a potash-lime glass. The choice between potash glass and soda-lime glass depends on the application: potash for optical and decorative work, soda-lime for windows and containers.

## Prerequisites

### Materials

- Hardwood (oak, maple, beech, elm preferred; softwoods yield roughly half the potash per volume)
- Iron or steel leaching vats and evaporation pans
- Water (soft or rainwater preferred; hard water introduces calcium contamination)
- Firewood or charcoal for furnace fuel

- Covered drying area for crystallized product (K₂CO₃ is hygroscopic)
- Supply of hardwood from managed forest or woodlot (roughly 1 acre of hardwood per kg of potash per month on sustained yield)

## Process Description

Potash production from wood ash follows three stages: controlled burning, water leaching, and evaporation crystallization. Each stage requires specific conditions to maximize yield and purity.

Hardwoods are burned in a controlled, oxygen-rich fire at 400-600°C. The goal is complete combustion to a fine white-gray ash without fusing the mineral content. Overheating above 800°C causes potassium salts to vitrify into an insoluble glassy mass, destroying yield. The ash is collected and aged for several days in a dry place; aging allows residual carbonates to complete formation from potassium oxide reacting with atmospheric moisture and CO₂.

The aged ash is packed into a leaching vat fitted with a false bottom and drain. Cold or lukewarm water percolates through the ash bed, dissolving potassium carbonate (solubility 112 g/100 mL at 20°C) and leaving behind insoluble calcium carbonate, silica, and char. The first run of lye is the strongest; subsequent rinses extract diminishing returns. The lye density is tested by floating a potato or egg (a fresh egg sinks in water but floats in lye with specific gravity above ~1.15, indicating adequate K₂CO₃ concentration).

The lye solution is transferred to iron pans and boiled to evaporate water. As concentration increases, the solution darkens and organic impurities surface as scum that is skimmed off. When the solution reaches supersaturation, K₂CO₃ crystals precipitate on cooling. The crude potash is drained and dried, yielding a gray-brown solid.

To produce pearl ash (refined K₂CO₃), the crude potash is redissolved in hot water, filtered to remove insoluble residues, and recrystallized. This recrystallization step can be repeated to increase purity. A single recrystallization raises K₂CO₃ content from ~50% to ~70-80%. Two recrystallizations achieve the 85%+ purity that qualifies as pearl ash. The final product is a white, slightly hygroscopic powder that dissolves readily in water to produce a strongly alkaline solution.

The evaporation stage also produces a valuable intermediate. As the lye boils down, a crust of less soluble salts (primarily sodium carbonate and sodium chloride) crystallizes first and can be skimmed off. This "salt cake" is a lower-grade alkali suitable for laundry and degreasing. The potassium carbonate, being more soluble, remains in solution longer and crystallizes last. This fractional crystallization effect is the basis of the pearl ash purification process: by controlling the evaporation and cooling sequence, different salts separate by their different solubilities.

The yield is modest. Roughly 1 kg of crude potash comes from each cubic meter of hardwood burned. This means a potash works producing 100 kg per month consumes the ash from about 100 m³ of wood, requiring several acres of managed hardwood forest on a sustainable yield basis. The enormous land footprint is why potash mining replaced wood ash leaching wherever geology permitted.

### Step-by-Step Procedure

1. Select and stack hardwood in the burn area. Use dry, seasoned wood split to uniform size for even burning. Avoid bark-heavy pieces (bark adds calcium contamination).
2. Burn the wood in a controlled fire. Maintain aerobic conditions (no smoldering). Target white-gray ash, not black char. Rake ash into a pile and let cool for 24 hours.
3. Age the ash for 3-7 days in a dry, covered container. Stir occasionally to expose all ash to air.
4. Pack aged ash into the leaching vat to a depth of 30-50 cm. Pour water over the ash bed slowly, collecting the drain-off (lye). Test lye density with a float test. Pass weaker lye through fresh ash for concentration.
5. Transfer strong lye to iron evaporation pans. Boil steadily, skimming scum and organic foam as it rises. Continue until the solution is thick and syrupy.
6. Pour concentrated lye into shallow cooling trays. Allow to cool slowly (12-24 hours). Collect K₂CO₃ crystals by draining excess liquid and drying crystals at low heat.

### Process Parameters

| Parameter | Range | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| Burning temperature | 400-600°C | Above 800°C causes salt vitrification, ruins yield |
| Leach water temperature | 20-60°C | Hotter water dissolves faster but also dissolves more impurities |
| Lye specific gravity | 1.15-1.25 | Below 1.10 is too dilute for efficient evaporation |
| Evaporation temperature | 100-110°C | Boiling point rises as concentration increases |
| Crystal drying temperature | 50-70°C | Too hot decomposes K₂CO₃; too slow allows moisture reabsorption |
| Pearl ash recrystallization yield | 60-70% per pass | Each pass loses K₂CO₃ to the mother liquor; recover by re-evaporating the drain-off |

## Safety Considerations

Potash production involves three hazard categories: furnace heat, caustic chemicals, and particulate exposure.

- **Alkaline burns**: K₂CO₃ solutions (pH 11-12) cause chemical burns on skin and severe eye damage. Lye splashes during evaporation are the most common injury route. Wear chemical-resistant gloves and eye protection when handling concentrated lye.
- **Furnace hazards**: Open hearth burning risks burns, carbon monoxide exposure, and fire spread. Maintain clear zones around the burn area. Burn only in ventilated spaces or outdoors.
- **Dust inhalation**: Dry potash powder irritates the respiratory tract. Handle crystals in still air or wear a dust mask during crushing and packaging.
- **Thermal burns**: Evaporation pans at 100°C+ and hot crystal drying surfaces cause contact burns. Use long-handled skimmers and tools.

### Personal Protective Equipment

- Chemical splash goggles when handling lye or concentrated solutions
- Leather or heat-resistant gloves for furnace work and evaporation pan handling
- Cotton or leather apron to protect against lye splashes
- Dust mask or respirator (P100) when crushing, grinding, or packaging dry potash
- Closed-toe boots with chemical resistance for work around lye vats

### Emergency Procedures

- **Skin contact with lye**: Flush immediately with copious cold water for 15 minutes. Remove contaminated clothing. Seek medical attention for burns larger than a palm. Do not apply acid to neutralize; the neutralization heat worsens the burn.
- **Eye exposure**: Irrigate with clean water for 15 minutes continuously. Do not rub. Seek immediate medical attention. Alkali eye burns can cause permanent corneal damage even if pain subsides quickly.
- **Fire in leaching area**: Potash is non-flammable, but surrounding wood structures are. Keep sand and water buckets at hand. Do not use water on hot oil or fat fires in nearby soap operations.
- **Spill containment**: Contain lye spills with sand or earth. Do not flush large quantities into waterways (alkaline runoff kills aquatic life).

## Quality Control

### Acceptance Criteria

- **Potash (crude)**: K₂CO₃ content ≥ 50% by weight. NaCl impurity below 15%. Moisture below 10%. Color: gray to brown (darker indicates more organic impurity).
- **Pearl ash (purified)**: K₂CO₃ content ≥ 85%. NaCl below 3%. Water-insoluble residue below 2%. Color: white to off-white.
- **Alkaline solutions**: Specific gravity 1.15-1.25. pH 11.0-12.0. Free of suspended solids after settling.

### Testing Methods

- **Float test**: A fresh egg floats at specific gravity ~1.15, indicating adequate K₂CO₃ concentration for evaporation. A potato works as a rougher proxy.
- **Acid titration**: Dissolve a weighed sample in water and titrate with standardized HCl using phenolphthalein indicator. Calculate K₂CO₃ content from acid consumed. The reaction is K₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2KCl + H₂O + CO₂. Each mole of K₂CO₃ consumes 2 moles of HCl.
- **Flame test**: Dip a clean wire into dissolved potash and hold in a flame. Potassium produces a distinct violet flame; sodium contamination shows as persistent yellow. View through cobalt blue glass to filter out the sodium yellow and see the potassium violet clearly.
- **Solubility test**: Pearl ash should dissolve completely in distilled water. Insoluble residue indicates calcium or silica contamination from incomplete leaching.
- **Gravimetric sulfate test**: Dissolve a weighed sample in dilute HCl. Add barium chloride solution. Any sulfate present precipitates as BaSO₄, which is filtered, dried, and weighed. High sulfate indicates sulfur contamination from the burning process (coal or sulfur-containing wood).

### Sampling Protocol

- Test lye density before each evaporation batch using float or hydrometer
- Titrate one sample per evaporation batch for K₂CO₃ content
- Flame test each batch of crude potash for sodium contamination
- Recrystallize pearl ash and verify purity by dissolution test before packaging
- Weigh all inputs and outputs for each batch to track yield and material losses. A well-run potash operation achieves 70-80% recovery of the potassium originally present in the ash

## Scaling Notes

- **Household scale (1-5 kg potash per burn)**: Single iron pot, manual leaching in a bucket, open-air burning. A single household hearth can accumulate enough ash over weeks for a small batch. No special infrastructure needed.
- **Workshop scale (10-50 kg per batch)**: Dedicated leaching vat (wooden or iron, 200-400 L capacity), multiple evaporation pans, covered drying racks. Requires a steady supply of hardwood from several acres of forest. Two to three workers. At this scale, counter-current leaching (running weak lye through fresh ash to strengthen it before evaporation) becomes practical and significantly improves fuel economy.
- **Industrial scale (tonnes per month)**: Multiple leaching vats in series (counter-current flow maximizes extraction), continuous evaporation furnaces, recrystallization circuits. Feeds directly into glass works or soap factories. Requires hundreds of acres of managed hardwood forest or access to evaporite deposits. At this scale, the operation might employ 20-50 workers across burning, leaching, evaporation, refining, and fuel supply.

Scaling challenges: leaching is rate-limited by diffusion through the ash bed. Deeper beds require longer percolation time. Counter-current leaching (freshest water contacts the most spent ash, strongest lye contacts fresh ash) roughly doubles extraction efficiency. Evaporation energy scales linearly with water volume; pre-concentrating lye by recirculating through fresh ash beds reduces fuel consumption.

At workshop and industrial scales, the evaporation fuel cost rivals the wood input cost. A batch that produces 50 kg of potash requires evaporating roughly 200-300 L of water, demanding 50-100 kg of firewood as furnace fuel. Using waste heat from the initial wood burn (the potash burn itself) to pre-heat leach water or supply part of the evaporation energy reduces this overhead. Some operations run the evaporation pans on the same fire that produces the ash, staging the process so that burning and evaporation proceed simultaneously.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Probable Cause | Solution |
|---------|---------------|----------|
| Low K₂CO₃ yield from ash | Softwood burned instead of hardwood, or ash overburned (vitrified) | Use oak, maple, beech. Keep burn below 600°C. Ash should be white-gray, not fused. |
| Lye specific gravity too low | Insufficient ash in vat, or too much wash water diluting the lye | Pack ash tighter. Use less rinse water. Recirculate weak lye through fresh ash. |
| Crystals dark or discolored | Organic impurities from incomplete skimming during evaporation | Skim more thoroughly. Recrystallize the crude potash by redissolving and re-evaporating. |
| Pearl ash high in NaCl | Salt contamination in leaching water or ash from coastal wood | Use rainwater or inland-sourced wood. NaCl co-crystallizes with K₂CO₃; fractional crystallization at different temperatures separates them. |
| Crystals dissolve during storage | K₂CO₃ is hygroscopic, absorbing atmospheric moisture | Store in sealed containers (glass jars, wax-lined barrels) in a dry location. Add a desiccant if available. |
| Evaporation too slow | Pan surface area too small, or heat input insufficient | Use wider, shallower pans. Increase furnace draft. Pre-heat lye before adding to the pan. |
| Leach vat drains slowly | Ash packed too tightly, or fine ash particles clogging the false bottom | Mix coarse charcoal chunks with the ash to maintain porosity. Install a coarser screen on the false bottom. |
| Pearl ash recrystallization yield below 50% | Cooling too fast (many small crystals form and pass through the filter); or too much water used for redissolving | Cool slowly over 24 hours for larger crystals. Use minimum hot water for redissolving (roughly 2:1 water to potash by weight at 80°C). |

## Variations and Alternatives

- **Sylvite mining**: Underground potash deposits (KCl) yield far more potassium per labor-hour than wood ash leaching. Requires mining infrastructure and flotation or solution mining to separate KCl from NaCl. Produces muriate of potash (MOP), the standard potassium fertilizer.
- **Langbeinite processing**: K₂Mg₂(SO₄)₃ ore provides potassium and magnesium without chloride. Preferred for chloride-sensitive crops. Requires flotation separation from associated halite.
- **Potassium hydroxide (KOH) production**: Electrolysis of KCl solution produces caustic potash, a stronger base than NaOH. Used for liquid soaps, alkaline batteries, and chemical synthesis. Requires chlor-alkali electrolysis infrastructure.
- **Seaweed ash (kelp)**: Historically, coastal communities burned kelp to produce a sodium-potash mix called "kelp ash." Lower K₂CO₃ content than hardwood ash but available without forests. Also yields iodine as a byproduct.
- **Synthetic soda ash (Solvay process)**: Na₂CO₃ from limestone and salt. Competes with potash in many applications (glass, soap) but does not provide potassium. If sodium-based alkalis are available, potash becomes primarily a fertilizer product rather than an industrial alkali.

### Material Handling

Potash and pearl ash are hygroscopic. Store in sealed glass, ceramic, or wax-lined containers. Keep off the ground and away from moisture sources. Label containers with product grade (crude potash vs. pearl ash), date of production, and K₂CO₃ assay result.

Crude potash can be stored for months if kept dry. Pearl ash degrades faster because higher purity makes it more reactive with atmospheric CO₂ (forming potassium bicarbonate, KHCO₃, which is less alkaline). Rotate stock using first-in, first-out.

Leaching vats must be rinsed between batches to prevent microbial growth in residual organic matter. Iron evaporation pans develop a protective oxide layer; do not scrub this layer off. Acid-clean pans only when buildup impedes heat transfer.

Leaching vat design affects yield more than any other factor besides fuel quality. A well-designed vat has a false bottom (a perforated wooden or iron plate) 10 cm above the true bottom, supported by bricks or a frame. The space below the false bottom acts as a clear collection zone where lye accumulates without mixing with settled ash particles. A drain valve at the lowest point allows lye to be drawn off without disturbing the ash bed. The ash bed itself should be 30-50 cm deep; deeper beds extract more potassium but require longer percolation time and risk channeling (water finding paths of least resistance and bypassing most of the ash). Some operations add a layer of coarse sand or gravel on top of the false bottom to improve flow distribution and filter fine ash particles from the lye.

The evaporation pans themselves are a consumable item. Iron pans in contact with hot alkaline solution corrode gradually. A typical cast iron evaporation pan lasts 2-5 years before developing pinholes. Wrought iron lasts longer but is harder to cast into large flat pans. Steel plate, welded into a pan shape, is the most practical modern option. The alkaline solution actually passivates iron to some degree (forms a protective magnetite layer), so pans in continuous use tend to last longer than pans used intermittently, which cycle between wet and dry states that accelerate corrosion.

Wood ash for leaching should be used within a few weeks of production. Aged ash left exposed to rain loses potassium carbonate through leaching into the ground (which is precisely the process being controlled in the vat).

Potash production by wood ash leaching is environmentally demanding on forest resources. A producing potash works in the 18th century might consume the annual growth from 50-100 acres of hardwood forest. Managed coppicing (cutting trees to the stump and allowing regrowth from the root system) provides a sustainable supply on a 15-25 year rotation. Without management, potash production leads to deforestation within decades, as occurred across the northeastern United States and eastern Canada before mining replaced the wood ash route. Any civilization relying on wood ash potash should plan the transition to mineral sources early.

The historical potash industry developed a distinctive seasonal rhythm. Farmers felled and burned hardwood during the winter when agricultural labor was available. They hauled the ash to centralized potash works in spring, where leaching and evaporation occupied the summer months. Pearl ash refining and packaging filled the autumn. The potash works often shared infrastructure with asheries that produced lye for soap, creating a combined alkali production facility that served both agricultural and manufacturing needs.

Different tree species produce ash with varying potassium content. Elm and beech yield the most potash per volume of wood (roughly 4-6 kg per cubic meter). Oak and maple are close behind. Conifers (pine, spruce, fir) yield roughly half as much potassium per volume and their resin content creates more tarry residue during burning. Birch falls in between. The practical implication: a potash operation should prioritize hardwood from deciduous species whenever possible. The choice of firewood directly affects the yield and purity of the product.

Wood ash contains roughly 5-10% potassium carbonate by weight, 25-40% calcium compounds, 5-15% silica, and 10-30% unburned carbon, with the remainder being moisture and trace minerals. The leaching process exploits the solubility difference: K₂CO₃ dissolves readily while CaCO₃ and silica do not. Any sodium present in the ash (from coastal wood or contaminated soil) co-dissolves with the potassium and contaminates the product, which is why inland hardwood from sodium-poor soils produces the best potash.
